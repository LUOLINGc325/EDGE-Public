# Transitive composition lifts: finite complexity and when words add power

## Abstract

We study an explicitly defined syntactic preorder generated by forward composition, equality identification, and transitivity of graph-indexed inequalities. For finite labelled graphs \(G=(S,E)\) and \(H=(Q,F)\), its certificates assign \((s_q,u_q)\in S\times\Sigma^*\) to every target vertex and require, for each edge \((a,b,i)\), a source path \(s_a\xrightarrow p s_b\) with \(u_a p=i u_b\). A synchronous automaton recognizes exactly the padded certificate words. With \(N=|S|\), \(n=|Q|\), \(r=|F|\), and \(m=|\Sigma|\), it has \(K=N^r(m+1)^n\) ordinary states, giving a word-depth bound \(K-1\) and a PSPACE algorithm. The decision problem is PSPACE-complete even over four fixed letters on partial deterministic graph pairs. It remains PSPACE-complete when both graphs are path complete and the reducible target has strongly connected components of size at most two. For strongly connected path-complete targets with \(m\ge2\), every certificate instead has depth at most \(B=n-1+\lfloor\log_m n\rfloor\); forced prefixes produce a target-dependent finite witness graph with at most \(Nn(B+1)\) vertices. This promised problem is NP-complete, already for a fixed binary three-state source. The unrestricted unary problem is also NP-complete. An explicit family requires ordinary words of exact depth \(2^k\), with the input-size dependence stated precisely. A second fixed-four-letter family forces every certificate to have a shared straight-line program (SLP) of size \(\Omega(2^k)\), with a matching \(O(2^k)\) certificate and explicit graph input length \(\Theta(k^2\log k)\). Supplied SLP certificates can be checked in polynomial time, and existence under a unary grammar-size budget is NP-complete; polynomial-size grammars remain sufficient in the unary and strong path-complete regimes. Finally, unequal composition depths preserve a common multiplicative Lyapunov rate after depth-dependent scaling. These results concern the specified syntactic closure and do not assert completeness for all semantic template preorders.

For strongly connected complete deterministic sources on at least two letters, we also characterize when every witness can be replaced by an ordinary graph homomorphism. This happens exactly when the letters are permutations. Every synchronizable subset of a strongly connected complete deterministic source gives a target whose minimum certificate depth is its shortest synchronizing length. We also give a reverse-deterministic collapse theorem and exact target-treewidth complexity bounds.

## 1. Results and relation to the motivating problem

An infinite composition lift can express useful consequences of finitely many graph-indexed inequalities, but its existence does not provide an algorithm for comparing finite certificates. The question considered here is concrete: given two finite labelled graphs, can the target be mapped into the countable graph defined by the source-path equation \(u_a p=i u_b\)? We give finite certificates, decide their existence, identify structural regimes with different complexity, determine when a standard grammar representation still requires large certificates, and connect the certificates to rate-preserving inequality transfer.

### 1.1 Source correspondence and its limits

Jongeneel and Jungers introduce composition lifts and, in Section IV-B of [JJ25], propose adding transitive consequences while retaining single-letter inequalities. That section leaves a formal study for future work. Their composition convention is translated explicitly in Section 2. The unit-rate soundness statement there motivates our algebraic interpretation, but does not supply the decision theorem proved below. The later abstract lifting theorem of [JJ26] is followed by a question about finite representations. Our results give an effective representation relative to each finite target for one precisely declared syntactic relation. They neither classify every abstract lift in [JJ26] nor produce one finite replacement valid for every target.

Ninite and Jungers [NJ26, §§III–V] use a forward lift that splits a vertex by its out-neighbors. Their Theorems 2 and 3 concern path completeness and feasible quadratic Lyapunov bounds. Their Proposition 3 and main algorithm use tight-edge information to decide where to apply this finite refinement. The comparison here concerns the distinct source-path word equation and the minimum certificate depth. The full text of [ADGMR26] was still unavailable from the publisher and institutional entries, and no theorem from it is imported.

The external complexity inputs are classical DFA-intersection PSPACE hardness [K77], ordinary graph three-colorability NP hardness [GJS76], and the standard space-saving reachability method [S70], whose relevant construction is described in Section 3. All reductions specific to composition certificates are proved here.

Ordinary simulation characterizations of path-complete methods [PJ19], template-dependent lifts including composition [DDRJ21], and effective comparison for addition-closed templates [DDRJ23] are established background. Their operations and quantifiers must not be conflated with the particular chronological word/path relation fixed here. Unit-rate composition soundness is therefore not presented as a new principle.

The algorithmic methodology also has explicit predecessors. Synchronized regular relations, products of atomic-relation automata, bounded guessing, and the resulting PSPACE method occur in automatic-structure theory [BG00, Definition 2.1 and Theorem 3.7(i)]. General word-equation and rational-constraint methods [DGH], and automaton-intersection reductions for restricted word equations [MNS16], provide further context. Section 3 specializes this methodology to the graph relation and supplies its one-letter-delay presentation and state count. The graph-specific pinning reductions, forced-prefix regime, and transfer statements must be evaluated separately from those classical methods; the generic automata strategy is not claimed as new.

### 1.2 Representation and terminology

Graphs and their vertex and edge sets are explicitly represented. The alphabet is finite and nonempty. Empty words and zero-edge source paths are allowed. Identical repeated edge triples can be removed without changing the problem. A graph is *partial deterministic* if every vertex has at most one outgoing edge bearing each label; transitions may be missing. A graph is *path complete* if every finite word labels a path somewhere in the graph, without a prescribed initial or final vertex. Promises of path completeness in Section 5 are not claimed to be polynomial-time recognizable.

The unanchored problem chooses all source images. Its anchored variant additionally prescribes some or all source images, while leaving the composition words unknown. The algorithms and finite upper bounds support either variant; hardness statements identify explicitly when no anchors are supplied.

## 2. Exact scope and a path normal form

Let \(\Sigma\) be a nonempty finite alphabet. A labelled graph is a triple
\(G=(S,\Sigma,E)\), where \(E\subseteq S\times S\times\Sigma\). Parallel
copies of an identical edge are immaterial. A path may have length zero; a
zero-length path starts and ends at the same vertex and has label
\(\varepsilon\). Words are written in chronological order. Thus, for maps
\(f_i:X\to X\),
\[
 f_\varepsilon=\operatorname{id},\qquad f_{uv}=f_v\circ f_u.
\tag{2.1}
\]
In particular, the tuple convention \((s,j_1,\ldots,j_T)\) in the composition
lift of [JJ25, Definition III.1] corresponds to our word \(j_T\cdots j_1\).
This reversal is a convention change, not an additional operation.

Terms are pairs \((s,u)\in S\times\Sigma^*\), representing
\(V_s\circ f_u\). Define the **syntactic source relation** \(R_G\) to be the
least relation containing
\[
 (s,u)\ R_G\ (t,ui)\quad ((s,t,i)\in E)
\tag{2.2}
\]
for every context \(u\), and closed under reflexivity, transitivity, and
common chronological prefixes: \((s,u)R_G(t,v)\) implies
\((s,ru)R_G(t,rv)\). The definition includes only these rules. It does not
include identities of particular maps, commutation assumptions, positivity
arguments, or all valid inequalities of an analytic template.

**Theorem 2.1 (path normal form).**
\[
 (s,u)\ R_G\ (t,v)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \exists p\in\Sigma^*:\ s\xrightarrow[p]{G}t\ \text{ and }\ up=v.
\tag{2.3}
\]

**Proof.** The right-hand relation contains (2.2), using the corresponding
one-edge path. It is reflexive by the empty path. If \(up=v\) and \(vp'=w\),
concatenating the two source paths gives \(u(pp')=w\), so it is transitive.
Prefixing \(up=v\) by \(r\) proves closure under the prefix rule. Minimality
of \(R_G\) therefore proves the forward implication. Conversely, induction
on a path with label \(p=i_1\cdots i_k\) applies (2.2) successively in the
contexts \(u,ui_1,\ldots,ui_1\cdots i_{k-1}\). Transitivity gives
\((s,u)R_G(t,up)\). The induction starts with reflexivity at \(k=0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Define the **transitive composition graph** \(T(G)\) to have vertex set
\(S\times\Sigma^*\) and a label-\(i\) edge
\[
 (s,u)\xrightarrow{i}(t,v)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \exists p:\ s\xrightarrow[p]{G}t,\quad up=iv.
\tag{2.4}
\]
Equivalently, its edge means \((s,u)R_G(t,iv)\). This is the precise fragment
studied here. The normal form makes the equality-generated edges explicit:
if \(u=iv\), an edge exists with \(s=t\) even when \(G\) has no edges.

For a finite target \(H=(Q,\Sigma,F)\), a **certificate** consists of source
states \(s_q\), words \(u_q\), and, for each \(e=(a,b,i)\in F\), a source
path \(p_e:s_a\to s_b\) such that
\[
 u_a p_e=i u_b.
\tag{2.5}
\]
Thus a certificate is exactly a label-preserving graph homomorphism
\(H\to T(G)\), together with witnesses for its edges. An anchored instance
additionally fixes some or all of the \(s_q\). All complexity statements
below concern the unanchored problem unless specified otherwise.

The normal form is complete for its declared syntactic rules. One can also
test that the rules do not secretly add other universal inequalities.

**Proposition 2.2 (a separating Boolean model).** If
\((s,u)\not R_G(t,v)\), there are a set \(X\), functions \(f_i:X\to X\),
and functions \(V_a:X\to\{0,1\}\) satisfying all source inequalities
\(V_a\geq V_b\circ f_i\), but with
\(V_s(f_u(\varepsilon))<V_t(f_v(\varepsilon))\).

**Proof.** Put \(X=\Sigma^*\), let \(f_i(r)=ri\), and define
\(V_a(r)=1\) exactly when \((a,r)R_G(t,v)\). If \((a,b,i)\in E\) and
\(V_b(ri)=1\), (2.2) and transitivity imply \(V_a(r)=1\); if
\(V_b(ri)=0\), the inequality is automatic. Reflexivity gives \(V_t(v)=1\),
whereas the hypothesis gives \(V_s(u)=0\). Equation (2.1) gives
\(f_u(\varepsilon)=u\) and \(f_v(\varepsilon)=v\). \(\blacksquare\)

This model is not asserted to be a positive-definite coercive Lyapunov
template. Proposition 2.2 proves no completeness theorem for such analytic
templates.


Throughout Section 3, write \(N=|S|\), \(n=|Q|\), \(r=|F|\), and \(m=|\Sigma|\). A prescribed anchor fixes a value of \(s_q\), not a value of \(u_q\).

## 3. Regular certificates and the general upper bound


**Theorem 3.1 (regular certificates and an explicit small-model bound).** Assume \(N\geq1\), and fix a source assignment \(s:Q\to S\). Set

\[
 K=N^r(m+1)^n. \tag{3.1}
\]

The canonical padded convolutions of the word assignments admitting paths in (2.5) form a regular language. There is an explicit nondeterministic finite automaton with one distinguished initial state and at most \(K\) ordinary states recognizing this language. If the language is nonempty, it contains a certificate satisfying

\[
 \max_{q\in Q}|u_q|\leq K-1,
 \qquad |p_e|=1+|u_b|-|u_a|\leq K. \tag{3.2}
\]

The maximum over an empty vertex set is taken to be zero. For an unanchored or partially anchored problem, the same bound applies without multiplying \(K\) by the number of source assignments: select any successful assignment and apply the fixed-assignment theorem.

**Corollary 3.2 (an effective finite representation for each query).** For fixed \(G,H\) with \(N\ge1\), existence of a certificate is equivalent to existence of a homomorphism

\[
 H\longrightarrow T(G)[S\times\Sigma^{\leq K-1}], \tag{3.3}
\]

where brackets denote the induced labelled subgraph. Source-path membership for each possible edge is a finite exact-word reachability calculation in \(G\). The cutoff depends on \(H\) as well as \(G\). The claim does **not** provide one finite graph depending only on \(G\) that works simultaneously for every finite target. Explicitly listing all vertices in (3.3) can be much larger than the automaton in Theorem 3.1; the decision algorithm below does not construct that list.

**Corollary 3.3 (space upper bound).** For the usual explicit representation of finite graphs, the anchored and unanchored decision problems are in PSPACE. The conclusion does not assume bounded word depth as additional input.

### 3.1 The synchronous automaton

Introduce a padding symbol \(\bot\notin\Sigma\). An input column is an element of

\[
 C=(\Sigma\cup\{\bot\})^Q.
\]

For words \((u_q)_{q\in Q}\) of maximum length \(L\), the canonical input has columns \(x^0,\ldots,x^L\), where

\[
 x^k_q=\begin{cases}u_q[k],& k<|u_q|,\\\bot,&k\geq|u_q|.\end{cases}
\]

Here indices start at zero. The final column \(x^L\) is all padding, and all earlier columns have at least one nonpadding coordinate. Even when every word is empty, the canonical input contains one all-padding column. This final column is necessary: the right side of (2.5) has one more initial letter than \(u_b\), so its final letter can be read one column later than the final letter of \(u_b\).

Besides a distinguished initial state \(I_s\), the automaton has ordinary states

\[
 (c,t)\in C\times S^F. \tag{3.4}
\]

The column \(c\) stores the previous input column. The coordinate \(t_e\) records the current source vertex on the path for edge \(e\). Before the first column, set \(t_e=s_a\) for \(e=(a,b,i)\). There is no actual previous column at the initial state. In particular, the initial state must not be identified with an ordinary all-padding state.

Given the next column \(x\), define its right-side letter for \(e=(a,b,i)\) by

\[
 R_e=\begin{cases}i,&\text{at }I_s,\\c_b,&\text{at an ordinary state }(c,t).\end{cases} \tag{3.5}
\]

This is exactly the next letter of \([i]u_b\), with a one-column delay for the coordinates of \(u_b\). A transition to \((x,t')\) is allowed precisely under the following rules.

1. At an ordinary state, if \(c_q=\bot\), then \(x_q=\bot\). Thus padding is permanent for each vertex.
2. If \(x_a\in\Sigma\), require \(x_a=R_e\) and \(t'_e=t_e\). This matches a letter of the prefix \(u_a\), without reading a source edge.
3. If \(x_a=\bot\) and \(R_e\in\Sigma\), require the source edge \((t_e,t'_e,R_e)\in E\). This reads one letter of the residual path word \(p_e\).
4. If \(x_a=R_e=\bot\), require \(t'_e=t_e\).

In the initial transition, use the initial values \(t_e=s_a\) in these rules. The requirements for all target edges are imposed simultaneously; the source-edge choices are independent coordinates of the nondeterministic transition.

An ordinary state is accepting exactly when its column is all padding and \(t_e=s_b\) for every \(e=(a,b,i)\). All ordinary all-padding states have no outgoing transitions. This makes acceptance occur at the first all-padding column, giving the canonical encoding with exactly one terminal flush column. A rejected all-padding state cannot be repaired by further padding: once the preceding column is all padding, every later right-side symbol would also be padding, so no source-path coordinate could change.

There are exactly \((m+1)^n\) possible stored columns and at most \(N^r\) source-path state vectors. This establishes the state count (3.1).

### 3.2 Correctness of the automaton

**Lemma 3.4 (soundness).** An accepting run produces paths and words satisfying (2.5).

**Proof.** The permanent-padding rule makes each coordinate of the input a finite word followed by padding. Because the accepting column is the first all-padding column, the input is precisely the canonical convolution of those words. Fix a target edge \(e=(a,b,i)\).

By (3.5), the sequence of nonpadding right-side symbols encountered during the run is exactly \([i]u_b\): the first symbol is \(i\), and the subsequent symbols are the letters of \(u_b\), read with one column of delay. The terminal all-padding column ensures that the last possible letter of \(u_b\) is included.

While the \(a\)-coordinate is nonpadding, rule 2 matches its letters against the right-side symbols, one for one. It therefore verifies that \(u_a\) is a prefix of \([i]u_b\). If a right-side symbol became padding before \(u_a\) ended, rule 2 would fail. The source-path coordinate remains \(s_a\) throughout this prefix phase.

Once the \(a\)-coordinate first becomes padding, it remains padding. Every remaining nonpadding right-side symbol causes exactly one source edge to be read by rule 3; rule 4 makes no change on padding. Concatenating the source edges chosen in rule 3 gives a path labelled by the remaining suffix \(p_e\) of \([i]u_b\), initially at \(s_a\). Acceptance states that its final vertex is \(s_b\). Consequently \(u_a p_e=[i]u_b\), as required. This argument also covers an empty residual suffix: then no source edge is read, and acceptance forces \(s_a=s_b\). Apply the argument to every target edge. \(\blacksquare\)

**Lemma 3.5 (completeness).** Every certificate satisfying (2.5) yields an accepting run on its canonical convolution.

**Proof.** Read the canonical convolution of the given words. Equation (2.5) guarantees the letter comparisons of rule 2 until each \(u_a\) ends. At that point the unread right-side suffix is exactly \(p_e\). Follow the given source path for that edge each time rule 3 applies, and leave the source-path coordinate unchanged when rule 4 applies. The padding condition holds by construction. All source paths finish at their required \(s_b\) by the terminal flush column, so that column is accepting. Choices for different target edges coexist because each has its own source-path state coordinate. \(\blacksquare\)

The two lemmas prove exact recognition. In particular, they establish regularity directly, without a general theorem about arbitrary word equations or rational constraints. For an unanchored or partially anchored problem there are finitely many permitted source assignments, so the union of their recognized languages is regular as well.

### 3.3 Extracting the explicit bound

Take a shortest accepting run for a successful fixed source assignment. It cannot visit the same ordinary state twice: deleting the segment between two equal states would preserve a valid run from the distinguished initial state to acceptance. Equality includes the complete previous column, so deletion preserves the one-column information needed by (3.5). Every surviving input remains a valid padded convolution by the automaton's transition rules.

An accepting run with \(T\) transitions visits \(T\) ordinary states, because the first transition leaves the distinguished initial state. Therefore \(T\leq K\). For a canonical convolution, its maximum word length is \(T-1\): the last transition reads the single flush column. Hence \(L\leq K-1\), including the case \(L=0\). The additional initial state is the reason to count transitions and the flush column explicitly.

Taking lengths in (2.5) yields

\[
 |p_e|=1+|u_b|-|u_a|.
\]

Nonnegativity is enforced by prefix matching. Since \(|u_b|\leq K-1\), the source-path bound is \(|p_e|\leq K\). An explicit certificate therefore uses at most \(n(K-1)\) word letters, \(rK\) path letters, and \(r(K+1)\) path vertices, in addition to the source assignment. These are upper bounds on actual uncompressed proof objects. They are not claims that all instances require certificates this large.

For partially anchored or unanchored inputs, apply the same construction to a successful source assignment. No extra factor \(N^n\) is needed in (3.2): that factor would count the number of assignments being searched, not the state count for the assignment witnessing existence.

### 3.4 Polynomial-space decision

Assume the vertex sets and edge sets are represented explicitly. A source assignment uses \(O(n\log(N+1))\) bits. An ordinary state uses

\[
 O\bigl(n\log(m+1)+r\log(N+1)\bigr)
\]

bits. The binary representation of \(K\), and of a counter up to \(K\), has size

\[
 O\bigl(r\log(N+1)+n\log(m+1)\bigr).
\]

A nondeterministic procedure first chooses the source assignment consistently with the anchors. It then generates at most \(K\) transitions, checking the padding, letter, and source-edge conditions locally, and accepts if it reaches the accepting state. It keeps only the assignment, the current and next states, and the counter. This is polynomial space; the words need not be stored.

For completeness, a deterministic implementation can enumerate source assignments and use recursive midpoint reachability in the implicit state graph. For each fixed assignment the accepting ordinary state is uniquely specified: all-padding column, with target endpoint \(s_b\) in the coordinate of each edge \((a,b,i)\). To test whether it is reachable within \(K\) transitions, enumerate a midpoint and recursively test two paths of half the allowed length. The recursion has depth \(O(\log K)\), and each frame stores a bounded number of state encodings and counters. Adjacency is checked directly by rules 1–4. This uses polynomial space, although time can be much larger. It is the standard space-saving reachability method underlying Savitch's theorem [S70].

The statement is about explicitly given finite graphs. A succinct input that merely declares exponentially many unnamed vertices using a binary count is a different representation problem and is not covered by this complexity claim.

### 3.5 Boundary cases

- **Empty target:** the empty assignment is a certificate, including when the source is empty. Set the maximum depth to zero and the effective state bound to one. The canonical convolution is the single empty all-padding column.
- **Empty source with nonempty target:** no assignment \(Q\to S\) exists, so the answer is negative before constructing an automaton.
- **No target edges:** if a source assignment exists, all words can be empty. Source vertices do not need self-loops.
- **Empty words on a target edge:** if both words are empty, (2.5) requires a source path of exactly one edge labelled \(i\). The initial all-padding transition checks that edge; it must not be treated as an empty path.
- **Empty source paths:** if \(p_e\) is empty, then \(u_a=[i]u_b\) and \(s_a=s_b\). Both are enforced by the automaton and by the independent checker.
- **Unary alphabet:** the same automaton, bound, and proof apply with \(m=1\). A stronger complexity classification can use binary-encoded word lengths; it is not needed for this upper-bound argument.
- **No path completeness or strong connectivity:** neither property occurs in the construction or proof.

## 4. PSPACE completeness and unavoidable long words

All hardness results in this section concern the unanchored problem. We import the PSPACE completeness of the nonempty intersection problem for a finite list of deterministic finite automata over a common alphabet. Kozen defines this problem as INT in Definition 3.2.2 and proves its PSPACE completeness in Lemma 3.2.3 of [K77]. The composition-specific reductions below are explicit.

### 4.1 Two loops remove the anchoring assumption


**Lemma 4.1 (empty-word pinning).** Suppose a target vertex \(x\) has loops labelled by two distinct letters \(r\ne s\). Every composition witness has \(u_x=\epsilon\). Its source image \(s_x\) consequently has one-edge loops with both labels \(r\) and \(s\).

**Proof.** The loop constraints give words \(p_r,p_s\) with

\[
u_xp_r=r u_x,
\qquad u_xp_s=s u_x.
\]

Taking lengths shows \(|p_r|=|p_s|=1\). If \(u_x\) were nonempty, its first letter would have to be both \(r\) and \(s\), which is impossible. Thus \(u_x=\epsilon\), and the equations reduce to \(p_r=r\) and \(p_s=s\). Because the relevant paths begin and end at \(s_x\), they are the required loops. \(\square\)

The conclusion concerns the entire word, not merely its first letter. It therefore eliminates a possible spurious witness using a long common marker prefix.

### 4.2 A direct intersection embedding

Let \(\mathcal A_1,\ldots,\mathcal A_k\), with \(k\ge2\), be epsilon-free nondeterministic finite automata over an alphabet \(A\). Write

\[
\mathcal A_i=(Q_i,I_i,F_i,\Delta_i),
\]

where \(I_i\subseteq Q_i\) is an arbitrary initial set and \(F_i\subseteq Q_i\) is an arbitrary accepting set. Rename all state sets so that they are pairwise disjoint. Acceptance of the empty word is allowed. Empty initial or accepting sets are allowed. If a chosen NFA convention includes epsilon transitions, first eliminate them using epsilon closures, including the corresponding changes to the initial and accepting sets; the reduction itself has only single-letter graph edges. For PSPACE hardness it suffices to use ordinary deterministic automata with one initial state, so epsilon elimination is not needed for that conclusion.

Choose letters

\[
\#,\$,r_1,s_1,\ldots,r_k,s_k
\]

that are all distinct and do not belong to \(A\). Define the source graph \(G\) as follows.

* Its vertices are the disjoint automaton states, new anchors \(a_1,\ldots,a_k\), and one shared terminal \(t\).
* Anchor \(a_i\) has loops with labels \(r_i\) and \(s_i\).
* For each \(q\in I_i\), add \(a_i\xrightarrow{\#}q\).
* Copy every transition of every \(\mathcal A_i\), using its original label in \(A\).
* For each \(q\in F_i\), add \(q\xrightarrow{\$}t\).
* There are no other edges. In particular, \(t\) has no outgoing edges.

The target graph \(H\) has vertices \(x_1,\ldots,x_k,z\). For each \(i\), give \(x_i\) the two loops labelled \(r_i,s_i\) and the edge \(x_i\xrightarrow{\#}z\). Vertex \(z\) has no outgoing edges.

**Proposition 4.2 (exact unanchored embedding).** The pair \((G,H)\) admits a composition witness if and only if

\[
\bigcap_{i=1}^k L(\mathcal A_i)\ne\varnothing.
\]

More precisely, in every witness,

\[
s_{x_i}=a_i,\qquad u_{x_i}=\epsilon,\qquad s_z=t,
\qquad u_z=w\$
\tag{4.1}
\]

for a word \(w\) in that intersection. Every such common word yields a witness with these assignments.

**Proof.** Lemma 4.1 forces \(u_{x_i}=\epsilon\). The source image must carry loops with both \(r_i\) and \(s_i\). Only \(a_i\) carries either of those marker loops, so \(s_{x_i}=a_i\). Thus the construction has enforced all anchor assignments internally.

The edge \(x_i\xrightarrow{\#}z\) now requires

\[
a_i\xrightarrow{\#u_z}_G s_z. \tag{4.2}
\]

The first edge of this path enters an initial state of the \(i\)-th automaton. Thereafter the path either stays in \(Q_i\), using only letters of \(A\), or takes one final \(\$\) edge from an accepting state to \(t\). It cannot reach an anchor, another automaton copy, or leave \(t\). Hence its endpoint belongs to \(Q_i\cup\{t\}\). Because the same endpoint \(s_z\) must work for every \(i\), the disjointness of the copies and \(k\ge2\) imply \(s_z=t\). Consequently \(u_z\) has the form \(w\$\), where \(w\in A^*\), and each path (4.2) gives an accepting run of \(\mathcal A_i\) on that same word \(w\).

Conversely, take \(w\in\bigcap_i L(\mathcal A_i)\) and use (4.1). The marker loop constraints are witnessed by the corresponding one-edge source loops. An accepting run in \(\mathcal A_i\), preceded by its \(\#\) entrance and followed by its \(\$\) exit, gives the source path with label \(\#w\$\). This satisfies (2.5) for the \(\#\) edge. There are no constraints leaving \(z\). \(\square\)

This proof includes \(w=\epsilon\): in that case \(u_z=\$\), and each required source path consists of a \(\#\) entrance followed immediately by a \(\$\) exit. The hypothesis \(k\ge2\) is essential for the common-endpoint argument. An input list with one automaton can be duplicated in polynomial time to meet it.

Let \(N=\sum_i|Q_i|\), \(D=\sum_i|\Delta_i|\), \(I=\sum_i|I_i|\), and \(F=\sum_i|F_i|\). The construction has

\[
|S|=N+k+1,\qquad |E|=D+I+F+2k,
\]

\[
|Q|=k+1,\qquad |F_H|=3k,
\qquad |\Sigma|=|A|+2k+2. \tag{4.3}
\]

It is explicitly polynomial in the input. When the input automata are deterministic with singleton initial sets, both source and target graphs are partial deterministic. Kozen's theorem therefore already implies PSPACE hardness for unanchored composition-witness existence on partial deterministic graph pairs, with the alphabet included in the input.

### 4.3 PSPACE completeness over four fixed letters

The separate markers in Proposition 4.2 are convenient, but they are not necessary for hardness. The next construction uses a fixed alphabet and still preserves partial determinism.

**Lemma 4.3 (binary coding without selfloops).** Given a finite list of deterministic automata over a common nonempty finite alphabet \(A\), one can construct in polynomial time partial deterministic automata \(\mathcal B_i\) over \(\{0,1\}\) such that:

1. their languages have a common word exactly when the original languages have a common word; and
2. no state of any \(\mathcal B_i\) has a one-edge selfloop with either binary label.

**Proof.** Put \(m=|A|\), choose

\[
\ell=\max\{2,\lceil\log_2m\rceil\},
\]

and fix an injection \(c:A\to\{0,1\}^{\ell}\), extending \(c\) to words by concatenation. For an input automaton with state set \(R\), use states \((q,v)\) with \(q\in R\) and \(v\in\{0,1\}^{<\ell}\). The initial state is \((q_0,\epsilon)\). Accepting states are exactly \((q,\epsilon)\) with \(q\) originally accepting. If \(|v|<\ell-1\), a bit \(b\) sends \((q,v)\) to \((q,vb)\). If \(|v|=\ell-1\) and \(vb=c(a)\), it sends \((q,v)\) to \((\delta(q,a),\epsilon)\); otherwise the transition is absent.

The accepted language is exactly \(c(L)\). Every transition changes \(|v|\) modulo \(\ell\); because \(\ell\ge2\), it never returns to the same state in one step. Thus neither binary label occurs on a selfloop. Each original state gives \(2^{\ell}-1\le4m-1\) coded states, so the construction is polynomial. A binary word accepted by every coded automaton splits into the same valid length-\(\ell\) blocks in each, and injectivity of \(c\) recovers a single common original word. \(\square\)

**Theorem 4.4 (fixed-alphabet PSPACE completeness).** Composition-witness existence is PSPACE-complete even when both graphs are partial deterministic over the fixed four-letter alphabet

\[
\Sigma_0=\{0,1,\#,\$\},
\]

and every strongly connected component of the target is a singleton.

**Proof.** Membership follows from Corollary 3.3. For hardness, start from a DFA intersection instance, duplicate an automaton if needed to obtain \(k\ge2\), and apply Lemma 4.3. Write the resulting disjoint binary automata as \(\mathcal B_1,\ldots,\mathcal B_k\).

The source contains their states, anchors \(a_1,\ldots,a_k\), and a terminal \(t\). Give every anchor both a \(0\) loop and a \(1\) loop. Add the directed anchor chain

\[
a_i\xrightarrow{\$}a_{i+1}\quad(1\le i<k),
\]

the \(\#\) entrance from \(a_i\) to the initial state of \(\mathcal B_i\), the copied binary transitions, and a \(\$\) exit from every accepting coded state to \(t\). No other edges exist.

The target has vertices \(x_1,\ldots,x_k,z\). Give every \(x_i\) both binary loops, include the directed chain \(x_i\xrightarrow{\$}x_{i+1}\) for \(i<k\), and include \(x_i\xrightarrow{\#}z\) for every \(i\). The sink \(z\) has no outgoing edges.

Lemma 4.1 forces every \(u_{x_i}=\epsilon\). By Lemma 4.3, no copied automaton state carries either binary selfloop, while \(t\) carries no loops. Therefore \(s_{x_i}\) must be one of the anchors. Write \(s_{x_i}=a_{j_i}\). Each target chain edge has empty endpoint words, so (2.5) requires a source path of exactly one letter \(\$\) between the two assigned anchors. The only such edges between anchors are \(a_j\to a_{j+1}\). Consequently

\[
j_{i+1}=j_i+1\quad(1\le i<k).
\]

Since all \(j_i\) belong to \(\{1,\ldots,k\}\), it follows that \(j_1=1\) and \(j_i=i\) for every \(i\). This proves unique source-state pinning, without assuming injectivity of the witness map or supplying anchors as part of the input.

The remaining \(\#\) edges now have precisely the form used in Proposition 4.2. After their first letter, their paths are confined to their own coded automaton copy until a final \(\$\) exit to \(t\). The extra \(\$\) edges on the anchor chain are inaccessible after a \(\#\) entrance, so they introduce no additional accepting paths. Therefore the common endpoint is \(t\), and \(u_z=v\$\) for a word \(v\in\bigcap_i L(\mathcal B_i)\). Conversely, any common coded word gives a witness, using the matching one-edge anchor chain paths as well.

Every source vertex has at most one outgoing edge per label: anchors have one edge for each binary symbol, one \(\#\) entrance, and at most one chain \(\$\) edge; coded automaton states are partial deterministic on binary labels and have at most one \(\$\) exit. The target has the same per-label uniqueness. Its only cycles are the binary loops, so all its strongly connected components are singletons. The whole construction is polynomial, completing the reduction from [K77]. \(\square\)

Corollary 3.3 and this reduction also establish PSPACE completeness for unrestricted graph pairs. Hardness over a binary alphabet or for strongly connected targets does not follow from the reduction. Path completeness, by contrast, can be imposed through the following construction.

**Corollary 4.5 (path completeness alone does not remove hardness).** Composition-witness existence is PSPACE-complete even when **both** input graphs are path complete and partial deterministic over the fixed four-letter alphabet \(\{0,1,\#,\$\}\), and every strongly connected component of the target has size at most two. The target is allowed to be reducible and disconnected.

**Proof.** Membership again follows from Corollary 3.3. For hardness, over the same four-letter alphabet, let \(C\) have vertices \(c_0,c_1\) and, for each letter \(i\), the two edges

\[
c_0\xrightarrow{i}c_1,\qquad c_1\xrightarrow{i}c_0.
\]

Thus \(C\) is complete and deterministic, supports every finite word as a path, and has no one-edge selfloops. Replace the pair \((G,H)\) in Theorem 4.4 by the disjoint unions \((G\sqcup C,H\sqcup C)\), with distinct vertex names in the two copies of \(C\). Each enlarged graph is partial deterministic and path complete.

Any witness for the original pair extends to the unions: assign the two new target vertices to their corresponding new source vertices and use the empty word at each. Every new target edge is then witnessed by the matching one-edge source transition.

Conversely, consider a witness for the unions. The two loops at each original target anchor still force an empty word. The added source component has no selfloops, and the coded source automata have no binary selfloops, so those target anchors still map to original source anchors. Their chain constraints again force \(s_{x_i}=a_i\). A source path beginning with \(\#\) from any such anchor stays in the original source graph, because the union is disjoint. The common-endpoint argument is therefore unchanged: \(s_z=t\) and \(u_z=v\$\) for a common accepted coded word. Hence a witness for the padded instance implies a witness for the original instance.

All original target strongly connected components are singletons, and the added component has two vertices. This proves the promised restriction. \(\square\)

The padding proves a complexity claim for path-complete graphs without a connectivity assumption. It deliberately uses a disjoint complete component and does not imply hardness for weakly connected or strongly connected path-complete targets. Replacing the two-state component by a single vertex with all-letter selfloops would be invalid: that new source vertex could receive every original target anchor and destroy pinning.

### 4.4 An exact exponential family of ordinary words

The hardness reduction does not by itself prove that shortest witnesses are long. We now give an explicit family with an exact minimum depth and an independent elementary proof.

For \(k\ge2\), let

\[
A_k=\{c_0,\ldots,c_{k-1}\}.
\]

For each bit index \(r\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}\), define a two-state partial deterministic automaton \(\mathcal C_r\). Its states are \(0,1\), its initial state is \(0\), and its unique accepting state is \(1\). Its transitions on \(c_j\) are:

| Relation of indices | Defined transitions of \(\mathcal C_r\) |
|---|---|
| \(j<r\) | \(0\mapsto0\) and \(1\mapsto1\) |
| \(j=r\) | \(0\mapsto1\) only |
| \(j>r\) | \(1\mapsto0\) only |

All unlisted transitions are absent. Define words recursively by

\[
W_1=c_0,\qquad W_k=W_{k-1}\,c_{k-1}\,W_{k-1}. \tag{4.4}
\]

**Lemma 4.6 (forced binary count).**

\[
\bigcap_{r=0}^{k-1}L(\mathcal C_r)=\{W_k\},
\qquad |W_k|=2^k-1.
\]

**Proof.** A simultaneous run has bit vector \(b=(b_0,\ldots,b_{k-1})\). The symbol \(c_j\) is enabled in the product exactly when

\[
b_j=0\quad\text{and}\quad b_0=\cdots=b_{j-1}=1. \tag{4.5}
\]

Higher bits are unchanged, bit \(j\) becomes one, and all lower bits become zero. For a vector other than the all-one vector, exactly one index satisfies (4.5): the least index carrying a zero. No symbol is enabled at the all-one vector.

For the integer value

\[
V(b)=\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}2^r b_r,
\]

an enabled transition changes the value by

\[
2^j-\sum_{r<j}2^r=1.
\]

The initial product state has value zero. The unique run therefore visits values \(0,1,\ldots,2^k-1\), and reaches the unique accepting product state after exactly \(2^k-1\) letters. It cannot continue. Splitting this unique run at the transition that first sets its highest bit gives recurrence (4.4). Thus the common accepted language consists of exactly that word. \(\square\)

Apply Proposition 4.2 to the list \(\mathcal C_0,\ldots,\mathcal C_{k-1}\), using separate marker pairs for the \(k\) target vertices. Denote the resulting pair by \((G_k,H_k)\).

**Theorem 4.7 (exact minimum ordinary-word depth).** Every composition witness for \((G_k,H_k)\) has

\[
u_{x_r}=\epsilon\quad(0\le r<k),
\qquad u_z=W_k\$,
\]

and its depth is exactly \(2^k\). In particular, a witness exists, but none can be represented by listing all its composition words using fewer than \(2^k\) alphabet symbols. The source paths required by its \(\#\) edges each have length \(2^k+1\).

**Proof.** Proposition 4.2 identifies every possible sink word with a common accepted word followed by \(\$\). Lemma 4.6 says that the only common word is \(W_k\), of length \(2^k-1\). The converse part of Proposition 4.2 supplies the witness. Finally the chronological equation for a \(\#\) edge is \(p=\#W_k\$\), whose length is \(2^k+1\). \(\square\)

There are exactly

\[
D_k=\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}(k+r)=\frac{3k^2-k}{2}
\]

copied automaton transitions. The constructed graphs have

\[
\begin{aligned}
|S_k|&=3k+1,& |E_k|&=\frac{3k^2+7k}{2},\\
|Q_k|&=k+1,& |F_k|&=3k,& |\Sigma_k|&=3k+2.
\end{aligned} \tag{4.6}
\]

Thus the depth is exponential in \(k\), and also exponential in the total number \(4k+2\) of source and target vertices. Both graphs are partial deterministic. The target is a star with two loops on each nonsink vertex and has only singleton strongly connected components.

The encoding qualification is material. This family contains \(\Theta(k^2)\) explicit edges, and conventional fixed-width binary edge-list encoding has length \(L_k=\Theta(k^2\log k)\). Accordingly, its depth is

\[
2^{\Theta(\sqrt{L_k/\log L_k})},
\]

not a proved \(2^{\Omega(L_k)}\) bound. It nevertheless rules out any polynomial bound on the number of symbols in a shortest ordinary-word witness as a function of this explicit input length. In a unit-cost edge-list measure of size \(\Theta(k^2)\), the bound is \(2^{\Theta(\sqrt{\mathrm{size}})}\).

The recurrence (4.4) also shows that these long words have short straight-line descriptions. No lower bound for compressed certificates follows from Theorem 4.7. Section 8 constructs a different family whose every certificate requires a large concatenation-only SLP. The present claim remains about ordinary words; neither representation lower bound proves an unproved complexity-class separation.

## 5. Strong connectivity and path completeness

A graph is **path complete** if every word over \(\Sigma\) is the label of
some path, with no prescribed starting or accepting vertex. Let
\(n=|Q|\geq1\), \(m=|\Sigma|\geq2\), and, for Theorems 5.2–5.4, assume
that \(H\) is strongly connected and path complete. Edge minimality is not
assumed. These properties are promises on the input; the complexity theorem
does not include deciding the promise.

The next lemma is valid for arbitrary finite labelled targets, without strong connectivity, path completeness, or a restriction to \(m\ge2\); this general scope is also used in the unary case.

**Lemma 5.1 (walk identity and forced prefixes).** For any target walk
\(q_0\xrightarrow{i_1}q_1\cdots\xrightarrow{i_L}q_L\), its certificate
paths concatenate to a source path \(p:s_{q_0}\to s_{q_L}\) satisfying
\[
 u_{q_0}p=i_1\cdots i_L u_{q_L}.
\tag{5.1}
\]
Consequently, \(|u_{q_0}|\leq L+|u_{q_L}|\); and, if
\(L\leq |u_{q_0}|\), the labels of that walk are the first \(L\) letters of
\(u_{q_0}\).

**Proof.** Substitute (2.5) successively and concatenate source paths. Taking
lengths yields the inequality because \(|p|\geq0\). Taking the first
\(L\) letters on both sides yields the prefix assertion. This also covers
\(L=0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Let
\[
 L_m(n)=\max\{k\in\mathbb N:m^k\leq n\},\qquad
 B=n-1+L_m(n).
\tag{5.2}
\]
The definition of \(L_m(n)\) avoids numerical roundoff in implementations.

**Theorem 5.2 (every certificate is shallow).** Every certificate satisfies
\[
 \min_q |u_q|\leq L_m(n),\qquad
 \max_q |u_q|\leq B.
\tag{5.3}
\]
More precisely, for a vertex \(q_*\) of minimum word length,
\(|u_q|\leq\operatorname{dist}_H(q,q_*)+|u_{q_*}|\).

**Proof.** Write \(d=\min_q|u_q|\). At any fixed starting vertex, Lemma 5.1
permits at most one distinct word of length \(d\) to label a walk. Path
completeness requires all \(m^d\) such words, so \(m^d\leq n\), proving
\(d\leq L_m(n)\). Strong connectivity supplies a shortest path from any
\(q\) to \(q_*\) of length at most \(n-1\), since a shortest path repeats
no vertex. Apply the length assertion of Lemma 5.1 along that path. \(\blacksquare\)

The next result avoids enumerating the \(m^B\) possible words.

For each vertex \(q\), construct a word \(h_q\), truncated at length \(B\),
as follows. Start with frontier \(A_0=\{q\}\). At step \(j<B\), inspect
all outgoing edges from vertices in \(A_j\). If they all have one label
\(i\), append \(i\) and let \(A_{j+1}\) be their set of destinations. If
at least two labels occur, stop. No empty frontier or empty set of outgoing
edges occurs: the hypotheses imply every vertex has an outgoing edge, and
each nonempty frontier therefore has one. Let
\[
 P_H=\bigcup_{q\in Q}\operatorname{Pref}(h_q),
\tag{5.4}
\]
where the empty prefix is included.

**Theorem 5.3 (polynomial finite witness graph).** Every certificate has
\(u_q\in\operatorname{Pref}(h_q)\). In particular,
\(|P_H|\leq n(B+1)\), and the finite induced graph
\[
 K(G,H)=T(G)[S\times P_H]
\tag{5.5}
\]
is constructible in polynomial time and satisfies
\[
 H\to T(G)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad H\to K(G,H).
\tag{5.6}
\]
It has at most \(|S|n(B+1)\) vertices. The graph depends on the target
\(H\); the result does not assert one finite graph valid for all targets.

**Proof.** Fix \(q\) and a certificate. At every depth
\(j<|u_q|\), all walks of length \(j+1\) from \(q\) have the same label
prefix, by Lemma 5.1. Since every walk extends, the frontier construction
cannot stop before \(|u_q|\), and its letters up to that point must equal
those of \(u_q\). Theorem 5.2 ensures \(|u_q|\leq B\). Hence \(u_q\)
is a prefix of \(h_q\). Counting prefixes proves the bound on \(P_H\).

There are at most \(nB\) frontier steps, each consisting of finite edge
scans. To decide a proposed edge \((s,u)\xrightarrow{i}(t,v)\) of (5.5),
first test whether \(u\) is a prefix of \(iv\). If it is not, no such edge
exists. If it is, the unique residual word \(p\) defined by \(up=iv\) has
length at most \(B+1\). Ordinary labelled reachability along that word tests
whether \(s\xrightarrow[p]{G}t\). All quantities are polynomially bounded
because \(L_m(n)\leq n-1\) when \(m\geq2\). The total output graph is
polynomial in the input size. Every homomorphism to \(T(G)\) lands in this
induced subgraph by the first assertion; inclusion gives the converse. \(\blacksquare\)

**Theorem 5.4 (NP completeness under the promise).** The certificate
decision problem with strongly connected path-complete target is
NP-complete. Hardness holds with the fixed binary alphabet \(\{0,1\}\)
and a fixed three-state source graph.

**Proof.** Membership follows either from (5.3) and direct path checks, or
from polynomial construction of (5.5) followed by a guessed homomorphism.
For hardness, use the source whose states are three colors, whose label-0
edges join each pair of distinct colors in both directions, and whose
label-1 edges are the three self-loops. Given a connected undirected graph
\(J\) with at least two vertices and no isolated vertex, let \(H_J\) have
both label-0 orientations of each edge of \(J\), and a label-1 loop at
every vertex. It is strongly connected. Every vertex has an outgoing edge
of each label, so it is path complete.

If a certificate word \(u_q\) were nonempty, the label-0 edge and label-1
loop leaving \(q\) would force its first letter to be both 0 and 1 by
(2.5). Thus all \(u_q=\varepsilon\). Each label-0 target edge must then
map to a one-step source edge, so its endpoints receive distinct colors.
Conversely, a proper three-coloring, with all words empty, is a certificate.
Ordinary three-colorability is NP-hard [GJS76], and remains NP-hard for the stated connected inputs: take
an arbitrary graph, add a new vertex, and join it to one representative of
each connected component. If the original is three-colorable, permute the
colors independently in each component so that all representatives have
color 1, and give the new vertex color 2. Restriction proves the converse.
The resulting graph is connected and has an edge. Trivial empty input
graphs may be handled separately. \(\blacksquare\)

### 5.1 The unary boundary

When \(m=1\), (5.3) cannot bound every certificate: adding a common unary
prefix to all words preserves every equation. Nevertheless, the general
unary problem admits a sharper complexity classification.

**Theorem 5.5 (unary NP completeness).** Over a one-letter alphabet, the
unanchored problem is NP-complete even for a fixed three-state source.
The NP upper bound also applies with prescribed source-state anchors.

**Proof.** A unary word is determined by its length \(d_q\). After the empty-graph cases of Section 3.5 are handled, the general
finite-automaton bound of Theorem 3.1 supplies, whenever a certificate
exists, lengths at most \(K=|S|^{|F|}2^{|Q|}\). Their binary encodings are
therefore of polynomial total size. Guess these integers and the states
\(s_q\). For \((a,b,i)\in F\), the required source path has exact length
\(\ell=1+d_b-d_a\). Reject if \(\ell<0\); otherwise compute the indicated
entry of the Boolean adjacency matrix raised to power \(\ell\) by repeated
squaring. This requires polynomial time in the input size and in
\(\log(\ell+1)\). It tests precisely the unary equation and path condition,
including \(\ell=0\).

For hardness, fix the source to be the loopless complete directed graph
on states \(\{0,1,2\}\), with the sole letter on all six edges. Replace
every undirected edge \(\{a,b\}\) of an instance of three-colorability [GJS76] by
both directed target edges. If a certificate exists, its two edge lengths
\(1+d_b-d_a\) and \(1+d_a-d_b\) are nonnegative, so
\(|d_a-d_b|\leq1\). If \(d_a=d_b\), the one-step source paths imply
\(s_a\ne s_b\). If \(|d_a-d_b|=1\), one of the paths has length zero,
so \(s_a=s_b\). In both cases
\[
 c(q)=(s_q+d_q)\bmod3
\tag{5.7}
\]
assigns different colors to the endpoints. Conversely, a proper
three-coloring with \(d_q=0\) gives a certificate. \(\blacksquare\)

If a unary target is strongly connected, subtracting the minimum word
length from every \(d_q\) preserves the edge path lengths. The normalized
certificate has \(\min d_q=0\) and \(\max d_q\leq n-1\), by Lemma 5.1.
For a disconnected target, this normalization can be done separately on
each weak component, but a linear bound then does not follow without
additional connectivity assumptions.

## 6. Exact multiplicative-rate transfer

Let \(X\) be any set, let \(f_i:X\to X\), and let
\(V_s:X\to\mathbb R_{\geq0}\). Suppose a common scalar \(\gamma>0\)
satisfies every source inequality
\[
 V_s(x)\geq\gamma V_t(f_i(x))
 \quad ((s,t,i)\in E,\ x\in X).
\tag{6.1}
\]
No topology is needed for the following algebraic assertion.

**Theorem 6.1 (rate-preserving reconstruction).** For any certificate define
\[
 W_q(x)=\gamma^{|u_q|}V_{s_q}(f_{u_q}(x)).
\tag{6.2}
\]
Then all target inequalities have the same scalar rate:
\[
 W_a(x)\geq\gamma W_b(f_i(x))
 \quad ((a,b,i)\in F,\ x\in X).
\tag{6.3}
\]

**Proof.** Inducting on a source path gives
\(V_s(x)\geq\gamma^{|p|}V_t(f_p(x))\). For the certificate path of an edge
\((a,b,i)\), evaluate this at \(f_{u_a}(x)\) and multiply by the positive
number \(\gamma^{|u_a|}\). The right-hand side becomes
\[
 \gamma^{|u_a|+|p|}V_{s_b}(f_{u_a p}(x))
 =\gamma^{1+|u_b|}V_{s_b}(f_{i u_b}(x))
 =\gamma W_b(f_i(x)).
\]
The first equality uses both the word equation and its length equality;
the second uses the chronological composition rule (2.1). Empty paths
obey the same calculation. \(\blacksquare\)

If a function template is closed under the indicated precompositions and
under multiplication by positive scalars, (6.2) belongs to that template.
At \(\gamma=1\), positive scaling closure is unnecessary. For analytic
Lyapunov conclusions one must also ensure that the resulting functions
are admissible. A sufficient concrete hypothesis on \(\mathbb R^d\) is
that every \(V_s\) is continuous, positive definite and coercive, and every
\(f_i\) is an origin-fixing homeomorphism. Composition then preserves
continuity and positive definiteness. It preserves coercivity because a
homeomorphism of \(\mathbb R^d\) is proper: the inverse image of a compact
set is the continuous image of that set under its inverse, hence compact.
Thus \(\|x_k\|\to\infty\) implies \(\|f_{u_q}(x_k)\|\to\infty\).
Positive multiplication preserves all three properties.

For arbitrary \(H\), the theorem proves inequality transfer. A stability
consequence additionally needs a path-complete target, admissible
functions and a decay condition such as \(\gamma>1\). The theorem does
not decide stability of an arbitrary switched system. The unit-rate
soundness theorem in [JJ25] does not itself establish the weighted formula
(6.2); the weights are necessary to cancel the varying source path
lengths.

For a switching sequence \(i_1,i_2,\ldots\), write \(x(0)=x_0\) and \(x(j)=f_{i_j}(x(j-1))\).

**Corollary 6.2 (a declared constrained-switching language).** Assume
\(Q\ne\varnothing\). Let \(L(H)\) be the words labelling some finite path
of \(H\), and put \(W_{\min}(x)=\min_q W_q(x)\) and
\(W_{\max}(x)=\max_q W_q(x)\). If a switching sequence has each finite
prefix in \(L(H)\), its trajectory satisfies, for every \(k\geq0\),
\[
 W_{\min}(x(k))\leq\gamma^{-k}W_{\max}(x(0)).
\tag{6.4}
\]
If \(X\) is a specified normed state space and common class-\(\mathcal K_\infty\) functions satisfy
\(\alpha_1(\|x\|)\leq W_q(x)\leq\alpha_2(\|x\|)\) for all \(q,x\),
then
\[
 \|x(k)\|\leq
 \alpha_1^{-1}\!\left(\gamma^{-k}\alpha_2(\|x(0)\|)\right).
\tag{6.5}
\]
In particular, \(\gamma>1\) gives uniform asymptotic decay for this
declared switching class. The class need not contain all switching
sequences.

**Proof.** Choose a target path carrying the length-\(k\) prefix. Iterating
(6.3) along it gives
\(W_{q_0}(x(0))\geq\gamma^k W_{q_k}(x(k))\). Bound the initial function
by \(W_{\max}\) and the terminal function below by \(W_{\min}\), then
divide by the positive scalar \(\gamma^k\). This is (6.4), and the common
bounds and monotonicity of \(\alpha_1^{-1}\) give (6.5). If \(\gamma>1\),
its right side is uniform over the permitted switching sequences,
tends to zero as \(k\to\infty\), and is at most
\(\alpha_1^{-1}(\alpha_2(\|x(0)\|))\), which tends to zero with
\(\|x(0)\|\). These are the asserted attractivity and stability bounds. \(\blacksquare\)

Thus reducible targets can encode constrained languages, but the control
application depends on which switching prefixes are covered by \(H\).
The PSPACE theorem concerns comparison of the specified certificate
graphs; it is not a complexity classification of deciding stability for
constrained systems.

## 7. A boundary of finite graph representations

The finite synchronous automata of Section 3 and the target-dependent
finite graph of Theorem 5.3 must not be confused with a finite graph that
works simultaneously for all target graphs.

**Proposition 7.1 (no uniform finite replacement in general).** There is a
finite source graph \(G\) for which no finite labelled graph \(K\) satisfies
\[
 \text{for every finite }H:\quad H\to T(G)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ H\to K.
\tag{7.1}
\]

**Proof.** Take a one-state source graph with no edges, over a nonempty
alphabet. Only empty source paths exist, so the edges of \(T(G)\) delete
the first letter: \((s,iv)\xrightarrow{i}(s,v)\). Its word length strictly
decreases on every edge. It therefore has no directed cycle, but contains
labelled directed paths of every finite length. If finite \(K\) has a
directed cycle, that cycle as a labelled target maps to \(K\) and cannot
map to \(T(G)\), contradicting (7.1). If \(K\) has no directed cycle,
every walk has length less than \(|V(K)|\). A target path with that many
edges, all bearing any one fixed letter, maps to \(T(G)\) but not to
\(K\). An empty \(K\) already fails for the one-vertex edgeless target. \(\blacksquare\)

This source is not path complete. Proposition 7.1 is deliberately a
boundary result for the full finite-graph domain, and is not presented as
a negative solution of the broader preorder question in [JJ26].

## 8. Compressed certificate representations

### 8.1 Representation and prior work

A shared straight-line program (SLP) over \(\Sigma\) is a sequence of rules
\(X_j\to\varepsilon\), \(X_j\to a\), or \(X_j\to X_hX_\ell\), where \(h,\ell<j\) and \(a\in\Sigma\). Each rule has one value, obtained by concatenation. A compressed graph certificate lists a source state \(s_q\) and an SLP root for each target vertex \(q\); different roots may share rules. Its grammar size \(g\) is the total number of rules. Root indices and source-state assignments are also part of the encoded certificate. Empty roots use an explicit epsilon rule; an empty target needs no rules.

This convention counts instructions, rather than the length of the expanded word. With binary indices, an SLP of \(g\) rules over a fixed alphabet takes \(O(g\log(g+1))\) bits. All lower bounds below concern this standard concatenation-only model. They do not exclude a small general-purpose program with loops, a formula containing a counter, or an alternative proof language.

Polynomial equality of SLP words is classical [P94, Theorem 13]. Regular-language recognition of compressed words is also established [PR99; BL26, Section 1]; we give the required matrix argument below. The grammar factor-counting argument is the mk lemma of [C05, Lemma 3], with the binary-rule convention made explicit. [BL26, Lemma 13 and Claim 14] constructs a factor-rich incompressible output of marked squaring, while [BGGG26, Lemma 10] gives an enumeration-word lower bound that also applies to superstrings. Finally, [DKM13, Introduction and discussion after Theorem 3.6] already explains why polynomial SLP witnesses for arbitrary regular constraints would imply NP=PSPACE. None of these generic facts is claimed as new. The graph-specific question here is whether every certificate, including alternative unanchored assignments and longer accepting words, is forced to contain such an obstruction.

### 8.2 Checking a supplied grammar

**Proposition 8.1 (compressed certificate checking).** Given explicit finite graphs \(G,H\), source assignments, and an SLP root for every word \(u_q\), validity of the graph certificate can be decided in deterministic time polynomial in the graphs and the compressed certificate encoding. Residual source paths need not be supplied as part of the certificate.

**Proof.** First check that all source images and terminal letters are valid, that each target vertex has a root, and that the rule dependencies are acyclic and in the declared order. Dynamic programming computes all word lengths in binary. Every rule has value length at most \(2^g\), so these numbers use \(O(g)\) bits.

For a target edge \((a,b,i)\), put \(v=i u_b\). Reject if \(|u_a|>|v|\). Otherwise extract from the SLP for \(v\) a prefix \(t\) of length \(|u_a|\) and the complementary suffix \(p\). An SLP for a prefix or a suffix can be obtained with \(O(g)\) additional rules: descend along the unique cut, reuse every entire child lying on the selected side, and concatenate the selected pieces. The descent visits at most one node at each grammar depth, at most \(g\) in total; zero-length children can be discarded. This construction uses binary length comparisons and does not expand any word. Apply the deterministic polynomial SLP equality algorithm [P94, Theorem 13] to decide \(u_a=t\).

It remains to decide whether the suffix \(p\) labels a path from \(s_a\) to \(s_b\). For every terminal \(j\), let \(M_j\) be the Boolean adjacency matrix of the \(j\)-edges of \(G\). Assign the identity matrix to an epsilon rule and the appropriate adjacency matrix to a terminal rule. Assign the Boolean product \(M_YM_Z\) to a rule \(X\to YZ\). Induction over rules proves that the resulting matrix records exactly the possible endpoint pairs of paths labelled by the rule's value. Thus the required path exists exactly when \((M_p)_{s_a,s_b}=1\). This includes empty paths: the identity matrix permits only equal endpoints. Evaluation costs polynomially many Boolean operations, for example \(O(g'|S|^3)\) for the augmented grammar of size \(g'\). Repeat independently for all target edges. Every accepted edge satisfies \(u_ap=iu_b\), and the suffix is the only possible residual word, so both directions follow. \(\square\)

The proposition is an algorithmic proof using a classical exact equality subroutine. The accompanying experimental code does not implement the Plandowski algorithm and must not be advertised as an executable polynomial-time compressed certificate verifier. Explicit expansion in finite tests has a declared size cap.

### 8.3 Forcing complete counter traces

Fix an integer \(k\ge2\). For \(0\le x<2^k\), let \(B_k(x)\) be its length-\(k\) binary representation, written with the least significant bit first. Define

\[
C_k=B_k(0)B_k(1)\cdots B_k(2^k-1),\qquad |C_k|=k2^k.
\]

For every bit position \(r\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}\), define a partial deterministic automaton \(A_{k,r}\) over \(\{0,1\}\). Its states are all tuples

\[
(p,e,c,b)\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}\times\{0,1\}^3.
\]

The coordinates record the next within-block position \(p\), the expected value \(e\) at bit \(r\), an accumulated carry \(c\), and the last bit \(b\) seen at position \(r\). Retaining unreachable tuples makes the counts exact and does not affect the language. The initial state is \((0,0,1,0)\). A state is accepting exactly when \(p=0\) and \(b=1\).

On reading \(a\in\{0,1\}\), first perform the following update:

| Position | Requirement | Updated registers |
|---|---|---|
| \(p<r\) | none | \(e'=e,\ c'=c\land a,\ b'=b\) |
| \(p=r\) | \(a=e\) | \(e'=a\mathbin{\oplus}c,\ c'=c,\ b'=a\) |
| \(p>r\) | none | \(e'=e,\ c'=c,\ b'=b\) |

If the requirement fails, the transition is absent. Finally set \(p'=(p+1)\bmod k\); if \(p'=0\), overwrite \(c'\) by 1 to initialize the carry for the next block. This describes at most one transition for each state and input letter. There are exactly \(8k\) states, \(16k-8\) transitions, one initial state, and four accepting states.

**Lemma 8.2 (counter-trace intersection).** The above automata satisfy

\[
\bigcap_{r=0}^{k-1}L(A_{k,r})=\{C_k^t:t\ge1\}.
\]

**Proof.** Every accepting word has length divisible by \(k\), since all automata advance the same position coordinate and acceptance requires position zero. The empty word is not accepted because every initial state's last-bit register is zero. Parse a nonempty potentially accepted word into length-\(k\) blocks.

In the first block, automaton \(A_{k,r}\) reaches position \(r\) with expected value zero; no earlier transition changes this register. Thus each bit of the first block must be zero. During any block \(D=(d_0,\ldots,d_{k-1})\), the carry register immediately before position \(r\) equals \(\bigwedge_{j<r}d_j\), with the empty conjunction equal to 1. The test at \(r\) checks that the current bit has its expected value and stores

\[
d_r\mathbin{\oplus}\bigwedge_{j<r}d_j
\]

as the expected bit at position \(r\) in the next block. This is precisely bit \(r\) of the binary successor of \(D\), with arithmetic modulo \(2^k\). Therefore the only possible successive blocks are \(B_k(0),B_k(1),\ldots\), cycling after \(2^k\) blocks.

At the end of a block, the last-bit register of checker \(r\) contains its bit \(d_r\). Simultaneous acceptance requires all these bits to be one, so it occurs exactly at the end of a positive number of complete periods. Conversely, every positive power of \(C_k\) satisfies each transition test and ends in simultaneously accepting states. This proves the equality. \(\square\)

This construction checks successive configuration blocks, a standard finite-automaton intersection mechanism. No new general intersection technique is claimed. Its purpose is to force a specific family of words in every certificate of the following graph instances.

### 8.4 Factor bounds and a matching grammar

For the words \(C_k\) of Lemma 8.2, let \(\kappa\) be the injective two-uniform code \(\kappa(0)=00\), \(\kappa(1)=01\). The following estimates apply to every word containing \(\kappa(C_k)\) as a contiguous factor.

**Lemma 8.3 (binary-rule form of the classical factor bound).** If an SLP has \(b\) binary concatenation rules, then the value of any one of its roots has at most \(b(\ell-1)\) distinct factors of length \(\ell\ge2\).

**Proof.** Unfold the grammar at the chosen root to a parse tree. For any occurrence of a factor of length \(\ell\), descend while all its positions lie in a single child. At the first node where the positions meet both children, the factor is a suffix of the left child's value followed by a prefix of the right child's value. This node corresponds to a binary rule. The length of the part in the left child is one of \(1,\ldots,\ell-1\). A rule and this length determine the factor uniquely, since each nonterminal has a fixed value. Thus at most \(\ell-1\) factors can be assigned to each of the \(b\) binary rules. Epsilon subtrees cannot be the crossing point, and a terminal subtree cannot contain a factor of length at least two. This is the standard factor-counting argument [C05], not a new grammar lemma. \(\square\)

**Lemma 8.4 (an explicit enumeration-word estimate).** Every SLP for a word containing \(\kappa(C_k)\) has at least
\[
\left\lceil\frac{(k-1)(2^{k-1}-1)}{4k-1}\right\rceil
\]
rules. Conversely, there is a shared SLP with at most \(3\cdot2^k+2\) rules whose roots include both \(\varepsilon\) and \(\kappa(C_k)\$\).

**Proof.** For each even \(x\) satisfying \(0\le x\le2^k-4\) and each \(r\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}\), the length-\(2k\) factor starting at offset \(r\) of block \(B_k(x)\) is
\[
F_{x,r}=B_k(x)[r:k]\,B_k(x+1)\,B_k(x+2)[0:r].
\]
Offsets are zero-based and intervals are half-open. Split \(F_{x,r}\) into its two length-\(k\) halves. Because \(x\) is even, \(B_k(x)\) and \(B_k(x+1)\) differ only in their first bit. The two halves therefore agree in their first \(k-r\) positions. They disagree in the next position, since the first bit of \(B_k(x+1)\) is one and that of \(B_k(x+2)\) is zero. The location of the first disagreement recovers \(r\). Once \(r\) is known, the middle full block \(B_k(x+1)\) can be read from the factor and recovers \(x\). Thus all \((k-1)(2^{k-1}-1)\) displayed factors are distinct.

The two-uniform injective code maps them to distinct length-\(4k\) factors of \(\kappa(C_k)\). A superstring retains all these factors. Lemma 8.3 gives the lower bound even if the grammar has additional roots or unreachable rules.

For the upper bound, create rules for epsilon, the two bits, and the two codewords: five rules. Reuse the codeword roots for length-one binary blocks. For every \(j=2,\ldots,k\), construct all \(2^j\) encoded length-\(j\) binary blocks by appending the encoded last bit to the appropriate length-\((j-1)\) block; this takes \(\sum_{j=2}^k2^j=2^{k+1}-4\) rules. Concatenate the \(2^k\) length-\(k\) blocks in increasing integer order using \(2^k-1\) rules. One terminal rule and one concatenation append \(\$\). In total this is \(3\cdot2^k+2\) rules. The estimate is an explicit version of the known enumeration-word obstruction; its application to all graph certificates is the additional step. \(\square\)

The lower and upper bounds are \(\Theta(2^k)\) in this family parameter, for rule count. They are not a claim of a sharp worst-case exponent in the total graph-input bit length, nor a lower bound for arbitrary computational descriptions.

### 8.5 A four-letter graph family with no small certificate grammar

Apply the fixed-length coding and anchor-chain construction of Theorem 4.4 to the \(k\) automata in Lemma 8.2. For this binary input alphabet, use the code

\[
\kappa(0)=00,\qquad \kappa(1)=01.
\]

Each automaton state \(q\) is replaced by three states \((q,\varepsilon),(q,0),(q,1)\). From \((q,\varepsilon)\), a first binary letter \(a\) goes to \((q,a)\). From \((q,0)\), the second bit \(a\) goes to \((\delta(q,a),\varepsilon)\), when that original transition exists. The states \((q,1)\) have no outgoing coded binary transitions because neither codeword begins in 1. Initial and accepting states use the empty-prefix phase. Every coded binary edge changes phase, so none is a selfloop.

For clarity, the unanchored gadget is recalled in full. In \(G_k\), create anchors \(a_0,\ldots,a_{k-1}\), disjoint copies of the coded automata, and a common terminal vertex \(t\). Each anchor has both binary selfloops. Add the chain edges \(a_r\xrightarrow{\$}a_{r+1}\) for \(r<k-1\), one \(\#\)-edge from \(a_r\) to its coded initial state, and a \(\$\)-edge from every coded accepting state to \(t\). The terminal vertex has no outgoing edges. All other edges are copied coded binary transitions.

The target \(H_k\) has vertices \(x_0,\ldots,x_{k-1},z\). Each \(x_r\) has both binary selfloops; the target contains \(x_r\xrightarrow{\$}x_{r+1}\) for \(r<k-1\) and \(x_r\xrightarrow{\#}z\) for every \(r\). There are no prescribed source-state assignments: this is the unanchored problem. Both graphs are partial deterministic over the fixed alphabet \(\{0,1,\#,\$\}\).

**Theorem 8.5 (unavoidable large grammars for every graph certificate).** Every certificate of \(H_k\to T(G_k)\) has, for some integer \(t_0\ge1\), exactly the vertex assignment

\[
s_{x_r}=a_r,\quad u_{x_r}=\varepsilon\quad(0\le r<k),\qquad
s_z=t,\quad u_z=\kappa(C_k^{t_0})\$.
\]

Every assignment of this form is a certificate. The graph sizes are

\[
|V(G_k)|=24k^2+k+1,\qquad |E(G_k)|=32k^2-1,
\]

\[
|V(H_k)|=k+1,\qquad |E(H_k)|=4k-1.
\]

In particular, the least possible ordinary certificate depth is \(2k2^k+1\), and every certificate word at \(z\) contains \(\kappa(C_k)\) as a prefix.

Let \(g_k\) be the minimum number of rules in a shared SLP certificate for this graph pair. Then
\[
\left\lceil\frac{(k-1)(2^{k-1}-1)}{4k-1}\right\rceil
\le g_k\le3\cdot2^k+2.
\]
In particular, \(g_k=\Theta(2^k)\). The lower bound applies to every certificate, including longer accepted powers and every possible unanchored source assignment.

**Proof.** At a target vertex \(x_r\), the two differently labelled loops force \(u_{x_r}=\varepsilon\): if it were nonempty, its first letter would have to be both 0 and 1 in the equations \(u_{x_r}p=0u_{x_r}\) and \(u_{x_r}p'=1u_{x_r}\). With an empty word, these two lift edges require one-edge source selfloops of both labels at \(s_{x_r}\). Only the anchors have them, since the coded copies have no binary selfloops and the terminal has no outgoing edge.

The target \(\$\)-chain now requires a length-\((k-1)\) path through source anchors, using actual one-edge \(\$\)-transitions. Among anchors these are precisely the edges of the length-\((k-1)\) directed chain. Hence \(s_{x_r}=a_r\) for every \(r\); no injectivity assumption is needed.

For a target \(\#\)-edge, its source-path equation reduces to \(p_r=\#u_z\). The first edge enters the designated coded automaton copy. The only common vertex reachable after these entrances from all the \(k\ge2\) disjoint copies is the common terminal \(t\). Thus \(s_z=t\). Such a path must finish with a \(\$\)-exit from an accepting coded state; no \(\$\) can occur earlier because the terminal has no outgoing edge. Consequently \(u_z=w\$\), where \(w\) is accepted by every coded automaton. The common fixed-length coding is injective and preserves their languages, so Lemma 8.2 gives \(w=\kappa(C_k^{t_0})\) for some \(t_0\ge1\).

Conversely, for each such power, use the source anchor loops and chain edges for the corresponding target edges. Each target \(\#\)-edge uses its entrance, the accepting coded run on \(\kappa(C_k^{t_0})\), and the final exit. These paths satisfy all original word equations.

There are \(8k^2\) original automaton states. The three-phase coding gives \(24k^2\) copied graph states. Adding \(k\) anchors and one terminal gives the vertex count. The coded transitions consist of two first-phase edges per original state, hence \(16k^2\), and one second-phase edge per original transition, hence \(16k^2-8k\). Add \(2k\) anchor loops, \(k-1\) chain edges, \(k\) entrances and \(4k\) accepting exits. The resulting total is \(32k^2-1\). The target has \(2k+(k-1)+k=4k-1\) edges. The depth and prefix conclusions follow immediately from the word characterization.

Every shared grammar must generate the root \(u_z\), whose value contains \(\kappa(C_k)\). Lemma 8.4 therefore gives the displayed lower bound for every certificate, even when other roots share its rules. Conversely, take the first accepted power and use the grammar from Lemma 8.4, giving its epsilon root to every anchor. This realizes the upper bound and proves the stated \(\Theta(2^k)\) minimum. \(\square\)

Under the explicit binary encoding of graph endpoints and labels used throughout the paper, these instances have size \(L=\Theta(k^2\log k)\). Thus the minimum shared grammar size in this family is \(2^{\Theta(\sqrt{L/\log L})}\), which is superpolynomial in the total input length. This is not a \(2^{\Omega(L)}\) bound or a sharp worst-case exponent for all graph inputs.

### 8.6 Bounded budgets and complementary regimes

**Proposition 8.6 (bounded-size existence).** Given explicit \(G,H\) and a unary budget \(B\), existence of a certificate having a shared SLP with at most \(B\) rules is NP-complete. Hardness holds already with the fixed three-state source and the strongly connected binary path-complete targets used in the graph-coloring reduction, for the constant budget \(B=1\).

**Proof.** A nondeterministic algorithm guesses at most \(B\) valid rules, all root indices, and all source images. Their encoding is polynomial in the input because \(B\) is unary. It then runs Proposition 8.1.

For hardness, use Theorem 5.4: a connected undirected graph becomes a target by giving each undirected edge both directed label-zero edges and every vertex a label-one loop. The fixed source has label-zero edges precisely between distinct colors and a label-one loop at every color. For connected graphs with at least two vertices the target is strongly connected and path complete. Each target vertex has outgoing edges with both labels, so a nonempty word there would have to begin with both zero and one; every certificate word is therefore empty. Certificates are exactly proper three-colorings, and one shared rule \(X\to\varepsilon\) suffices. Connected three-colorability remains NP-hard by linking components through new edges and permuting colors independently; isolated trivial inputs can be handled directly. This proves hardness for the promised subclass and hence for the general problem. The fixed source is not partial deterministic; no such restriction is claimed for this NP-hardness statement. \(\square\)

**Proposition 8.7 (complementary small-grammar regimes).** Every satisfiable unary instance has a certificate with polynomially many shared SLP rules. Every satisfiable strongly connected path-complete instance with \(m\ge2\) has a polynomial-size explicit certificate, and hence a polynomial-size SLP certificate. In unrestricted instances a general exponential-size SLP upper bound follows from the finite certificate theorem, whereas the preceding graph family rules out a universal polynomial bound in this SLP model, unconditionally.

**Proof.** In the unary case, Theorem 3.1 supplies words of length at most \(K-1\), where \(K=N^r2^n\). Precompute powers \(a^{2^j}\) by doubling for \(O(\log K)\) rules, and form each of the \(n\) required powers using its binary expansion and \(O(\log K)\) further concatenations. Include epsilon for zero lengths. Thus \(O(n\log K)\) rules suffice, a polynomial in the explicit input size. Empty graphs are treated separately as in the finite certificate theorem.

For the strong path-complete regime, the established bound \(B_H=n-1+\lfloor\log_m n\rfloor\) gives at most \(nB_H\) letters in the target words of every certificate. Literal concatenation constructs a grammar of polynomial size. In the unrestricted case the general bound gives total word length at most \(n(K-1)\), which yields an exponential grammar upper bound by literal concatenation. The explicit lower-bound family proves the unconditional failure of any universal polynomial SLP-size bound. These are regime statements; together they do not classify all graph subclasses or all compression languages. \(\square\)

The representation theorem applies a known grammar obstruction to every solution of the specified graph predicate. Polynomial compressed equality, regular-language matrix evaluation, factor counting, and the prior conditional NP=PSPACE barrier remain credited ingredients. The new graph-family result is unconditional for concatenation-only SLP size. It neither proves a complexity-class separation nor excludes short descriptions in richer programming languages. Its scholarly significance is assessed separately from correctness and implementation checks.

## 9. Executable certificates, examples, and validation

### 9.1 Implementations and proof objects

The implementation uses Python's standard library. [The general solver](evidence/code/certificate_solver.py) performs exact breadth-first search in the synchronous automaton and enumerates source assignments. It reconstructs all words and source paths, and returns a minimum-depth witness when the search finishes positively. This explicit implementation can use exponential memory; the polynomial-space decision argument is the separate implicit reachability construction in Section 3. A state budget is operational: exhausting it yields `unknown_resource_limit`, never `unsat`.

[The forced-prefix solver](evidence/code/forced_prefix.py) constructs the prefix lists of Theorem 5.3 and searches the resulting finite constraint problem. It can also export the full graph \(T(G)[S\times P_H]\). Its candidate construction is polynomial; finding a homomorphism remains NP-complete. By default, the prototype separately validates path completeness through exact subset reachability, which can be exponential. With `--assume-path-complete`, it skips that test and marks the answer conditional on the supplied promise. Strong connectivity is checked directly. A failed promise is reported as `promise_unsatisfied`.

[The independent positive-certificate checker](evidence/code/verify_certificate.py) imports neither solver. It verifies the input binding, source images, anchors, every recorded source-path edge, and the original equality \(u_a p=i u_b\). It verifies existence, not a solver's minimum-depth or negative assertion. Zero-length paths have one recorded vertex; they never join distinct source vertices. The [code guide](evidence/README.md) documents the input and output schemas.

### 9.2 A depth-one certificate and a depth-three representation

Take a two-state source with states \(A=0,B=1\). From either state, label 0 goes to \(A\) and label 1 goes to \(B\). The target contains this two-state core and an additional vertex \(C\), with edges

\[
 A\xrightarrow0 C,\qquad A\xrightarrow1 C,\qquad C\xrightarrow0 A.
\]

The target is strongly connected, and its complete two-state core makes it path complete. Set \(s_A=0,s_B=1,s_C=0\), \(u_A=u_B=\varepsilon\), and \(u_C=0\). The two added incoming edges use source words \(00\) and \(10\), respectively. The edge from \(C\) to \(A\) uses the empty source path, since \(0\varepsilon=0\varepsilon\). All core edges use their original one-edge source transitions. This is a depth-one certificate.

A depth-zero certificate would be an ordinary labelled homomorphism. The two incoming labels at \(C\) would then force its source image to be both reset states 0 and 1. Hence depth zero is impossible and the minimum is exactly one. This example exhibits a concrete consequence of allowing composition and equality-generated edges.

For the example stored in [the instance file](evidence/code/instances/forced_prefix_nonempty.json), replace \(C\) by a chain \(C_3\xrightarrow0 C_2\xrightarrow0 C_1\xrightarrow0 A\), and let both added edges from \(A\) enter \(C_3\). The assignment \(u_{C_j}=0^j\), \(s_{C_j}=0\) gives a valid depth-three certificate: the chain edges use empty source paths and the two entrances use words \(0^4\) and \(10^3\). The forced-prefix words are exactly \(h_{C_j}=0^j\), while \(h_A=h_B=\varepsilon\). Thus \(P_H=\{\varepsilon,0,00,000\}\), and the exported finite witness graph has eight vertices.

The depth-three representation is deliberately not claimed minimal. The solver also finds a depth-one certificate for this five-vertex target; both the depth-one and depth-three witnesses pass the direct checker. The [recorded certificate](evidence/results/forced_prefix_nonempty_certificate.json), [exported graph](evidence/results/forced_prefix_finite_graph.json), and [verification result](evidence/results/forced_prefix_nonempty_verification.json) make the example inspectable.

### 9.4 Counter-trace and grammar checks

The secondary-round counter checker constructs the DFAs of Lemma 8.2 and explores the complete reachable product component for \(k=2,\ldots,10\). Each tested component has one allowed successor at every reachable state, a transient of \(k\) symbols, period \(k2^k\), and first simultaneous acceptance at \(k2^k\). ## 10. Target treewidth and exact minimum depth

Assume the target $H$ is strongly connected and path complete, $n=|V(H)|\ge1$, $N=|V(G)|\ge1$ and the alphabet has $m\ge2$ letters. Set $B=n-1+\lfloor\log_m n\rfloor$. The case of an empty source is immediately infeasible for a nonempty target. Let $w$ be the treewidth of the target's undirected graph after removing loops, and suppose a width-$w$ decomposition is supplied.

**Theorem 10.1.** For each depth limit $0\le d\le B$, one can decide feasibility and reconstruct a witness in $[N(d+1)]^{w+1}$ times a polynomial in the input length and decomposition size. Minimum feasible depth is obtained by binary search over the limit.

For each target vertex $q$, use the candidate domain $V(G)\times\operatorname{Pref}_{\le d}(h_q)$, where $h_q$ is the forced prefix from Theorem 5.3. This domain has at most $N(d+1)$ values and contains every valid vertex assignment within the depth limit. For each target edge $q\xrightarrow{i} r$, two assigned values $(s,u),(t,v)$ are consistent exactly when $iv=up$ for a suffix $p$ and $G$ contains an $s$-to-$t$ path labeled $p$. The suffix test and reachability follow by a linear scan of the explicit words and propagation of a set of states along the letters of $p$. Intersect the conditions for parallel edges and treat a loop as a unary condition.

On a standard rooted tree decomposition, assign each such condition to a bag containing its endpoints. A table entry states whether the proposed bag assignment extends to all constraints placed in its rooted subtree. Introducing a vertex adds its coordinate, forgetting a vertex projects existentially to the smaller bag, and a join takes the conjunction for the same bag assignment. Check constraints assigned to the current bag before storing the entry. The connected-bag condition separates the two sides of a join outside the bag and guarantees this table invariant by induction. Each bag has at most $[N(d+1)]^{w+1}$ entries. Projection can be performed by scanning the larger child table, and joins by matching identical tuples. The remaining factors are polynomial. Recording witnesses at projections allows reconstruction. Feasibility is monotone in $d$, and Section 5 guarantees $B$ bounds every witness.

**Theorem 10.2.** On strongly connected path-complete binary targets, feasibility is W[1]-complete in $n$. In particular it is W[1]-hard in $w$. Under ETH there is no algorithm with running time $f(w)N^{o(w)}$ times a polynomial in the input length, even when both graphs are strongly connected and path complete, every witness has depth zero, and the target decomposition is given.

For hardness, reduce Clique on a nonempty undirected graph $X$ with requested size $k\ge2$. Add a universal vertex to obtain $Y$. It contains a $(k+1)$-clique exactly when $X$ contains a $k$-clique. As source, give $Y$ a 1-loop at each vertex and replace each undirected edge by two oppositely directed 0-edges. Give the $(k+1)$-clique target the same label construction. Both graphs are strongly connected and have an outgoing edge of each letter at each vertex. They are therefore path complete. The two distinct possible first letters force every target delay to be empty. A depth-zero witness is exactly a graph homomorphism from the clique into $Y$. Absence of 0-loops makes it injective. The target has width $k$ and its single bag is a width-$k$ decomposition. The source order grows by one, and the full encoding is polynomial. The standard Clique exponent lower bound therefore transfers. [Chen, Huang, Kanj and Xia, Theorem 5.5](https://www.cs.lafayette.edu/~gexia/research/jcss72_8.pdf)

For W[1] membership in $n$, use the finite prefix domains from Theorem 5.3, now with depth bound $B$. Construct a multipartite graph with one part per target vertex and one vertex per candidate passing its unary conditions. Connect candidates for distinct target vertices exactly when they satisfy every condition between those target vertices in both directions. A clique of size $n$ then contains one vertex per part and gives a consistent assignment, and any assignment gives such a clique. This is a parameterized reduction to Clique. It does not assert W[1] membership with parameter $w$ alone. Original width-bound predecessor · First continuation

## 11. When words add power

Write $\delta_u(x)$ for reading the letters of $u$ in chronological order in a deterministic complete source. For example $\delta_{uv}=\delta_v\circ\delta_u$. The maximum length among the vertex words is the witness depth. At depth zero the word equation forces each source path to consist of exactly the target edge's letter, so the assignments are exactly ordinary label-preserving homomorphisms.

### 11.1. Permutation sources

Suppose every source letter acts as a permutation. Given any witness for any finite target, set $h_q=\delta_{u_q}^{-1}(s_q)$. For a target edge $q\xrightarrow{i}r$ with witness path word $p$,
$$
\delta_{u_r}(\delta_i(h_q))=\delta_{iu_r}(h_q)
=\delta_{u_qp}(h_q)=\delta_p(s_q)=s_r.
$$
Applying $\delta_{u_r}^{-1}$ gives a homomorphism. The converse uses empty words. Thus every witness collapses to depth zero, even if the target is not strongly connected or path complete.

For decision and counting, take a spanning tree of the undirected graph of each weak target component. Fix one root image and propagate the assigned state along the tree using a forward permutation or its inverse, according to the chosen edge direction. Check every remaining edge and loop. Each root choice gives at most one map on the component, and each distinct successful root image gives a distinct map. With indexed permutations this takes $O(N(|V(H)|+|E(H)|))$ for decision and the separate successful-root counts. The number of global maps is the product of the component counts, with the corresponding integer bit cost. Listing the maps also has an output-size cost.

### 11.2. A necessary condition on recurrent deterministic sources

**Theorem 11.1.** Let $G$ be nonempty, finite, deterministic, complete, and strongly connected, on an alphabet of at least two letters. Composition feasibility implies ordinary homomorphism feasibility for every strongly connected path-complete target if and only if every source letter is a permutation. More generally, suppose $P$ is a subset of at least two source states with shortest synchronizing word length $d$. There is a strongly connected path-complete target of order $|V(G)|+d$ whose minimum composition witness depth is exactly $d$.

An endomorphism of $G$ is an automorphism. Indeed it commutes with every letter transition. Its image contains the orbit of any image vertex under all words, which is the entire source by strong connectivity. Finiteness makes it injective, and its inverse also commutes with the letters.

Choose a shortest synchronizing word $w=i_1\cdots i_d$ of $P$ and denote its common endpoint by $z$. Start the target with an exact copy of $G$. For each $a\in P$ choose a source edge $b_a\xrightarrow{j_a}a$, which exists by strong connectivity. Add a new $q$ and one edge $b_a\xrightarrow{j_a}q$ for each such choice. Add a path from $q$ to the old vertex $z$ labeled $w$, with $d-1$ fresh internal vertices. Every new vertex is accessible from and can return to the original copy. The copy allows every finite word, and the new target has the claimed order.

Each old vertex retains outgoing edges of at least two different letters. Its delay is empty, since a nonempty delay would have to begin with both labels. The old state assignments therefore form a source endomorphism $e$, hence an automorphism. For every incoming edge to $q$, the word equation gives
$$
s_q=\delta_{j_a u_q}(e(b_a))=\delta_{u_q}(e(a))=e(\delta_{u_q}(a)).
$$
Injectivity of $e$ forces $u_q$ to synchronize $P$, so the depth is at least $d$. For a matching witness use the identity on the old copy. At each fresh path vertex place source state $z$ and the suffix of $w$ remaining along the new path. The path edges use empty source paths since the previous suffix is the edge label followed by the next suffix. The incoming edges use source words $j_a w$ from $b_a$ to $z$. All old edges keep their original one-letter paths. This is a depth-$d$ witness.

If a letter is noninjective, choose two states in one fiber and take $d=1$. The resulting feasible target has no depth-zero witness. If all letters are injective, finiteness makes them permutations, and §11.1 applies. For example, let letter 0 send both states to $A$ and let letter 1 swap $A,B$. Add $q$ with edges of both labels from $A$ and a 0-edge back to $A$. The old 0-loop forces $A$'s image under a homomorphism to be $A$. The two incoming requirements then force $q$ to have both images $A$ and $B$. The witness instead assigns $(A,0)$ to $q$ with the identity assignments on the copy.

### 11.3. Reverse-deterministic sources

**Theorem 11.2.** Suppose $G$ has at most one incoming edge with any given label at any vertex. Missing transitions and forward branching are allowed. If every target vertex is reachable from a directed cycle, every witness collapses to depth zero.

For a target vertex $q$, use an incoming walk ending at $q$, of length at least the largest vertex-delay length in the witness. Such a walk exists by following a cycle sufficiently many times and then following a path to $q$. Concatenating its edge equations gives $u_{\rm start}p=vu_q$, where $v$ labels the walk and $p$ labels a genuine source path ending at $s_q$. Because $|v|\ge |u_{\rm start}|$, the suffix $u_q$ occurs inside $p$. Hence there exists a source state $h_q$ that reads $u_q$ into $s_q$. Its uniqueness follows by reading the letters backwards using the incoming-edge condition.

For a target edge $q\xrightarrow{i}r$, concatenate this predecessor path with the edge's witness path. The resulting word is $iu_r$, ending at $s_r$. The suffix $u_r$ must start at the unique state $h_r$, giving an actual $i$-edge from $h_q$ to $h_r$. This yields a homomorphism. The reachability condition cannot be dropped in general. With an edgeless one-state source and a target consisting of one directed edge of label $i$, assign delay $i$ at its tail and the empty word at its head, with the same state and empty path. The target has no ordinary homomorphism. For a nontrivial strongly connected target one also obtains a polynomial decision algorithm. Fix a root image, propagate backwards along an in-tree toward the root using the unique incoming edge of the required label, and test all remaining constraints. Try all root images.

Jongeneel and Jungers's transitive lift is a comparison construction for Lyapunov certificates. Their §IV-B and Theorem IV.5 do not state this source classification. Graph homomorphism and ordinary synchronization are established tools. Vorel bounds synchronization lengths and proves complexity results for subsets of strongly connected automata. The new construction here realizes a specified shortest synchronization length as *the actual minimum lift depth*, by pinning the recurrent copy. It needs $d$ extra vertices and does not claim a polynomial reduction when $d$ is exponential. The word-removal argument for reverse-deterministic sources also proves existence of the required preimages in the presence of missing transitions. The earlier positive-depth example in Section 9 gives one instance, while Theorem 11.1 gives a complete deterministic-source criterion. [Jongeneel and Jungers, §IV-B](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.18189v2#S4.SS2) · [Vorel, §§2.3 and 5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1403.3972) · Independent r02 review

## 12. Scope of the results


The finite-word bound is uniform for each fixed finite query, and the forced-prefix result gives a polynomial graph only under the stated target promise. The general exponential-state bound is not claimed numerically optimal. The first counter family concerns ordinary words and is compressible. The distinct counter-trace family of Theorem 8.5 forces a superpolynomial number of SLP rules in every certificate, with matching family-parameter bounds and honest explicit-input accounting. Its restriction to concatenation-only grammars is essential: no bound for arbitrary program descriptions, sharp worst-case input-length exponent, or complexity-class separation is claimed. Fixed-binary PSPACE hardness, hardness under weakly connected path-complete targets, and a universal classification of template preorders are not established here.

The transfer theorem concerns a common positive scalar and the declared forward-composition rules. It does not include edge-dependent rates, inverse-composition inference, functional commutation identities, or accidental equality between distinct source functions. The analytic corollary applies only to switching prefixes covered by the declared target language and to functions satisfying the stated bounds. The graph-comparison complexity results are not complexity classifications of switched-system stability.

The structural theorems in §§10–11 concern the path and word certificate problem with the displayed source and target hypotheses. They neither change Section 6's analytic assumptions nor settle semantic completeness for all Lyapunov templates. The original finite verifiers cover their stated historical scope. Full formalization of the integrated proof is a separate obligation.
