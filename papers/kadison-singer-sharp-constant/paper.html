Matrix discrepancy on two-dimensional subspaces and sharp variance-profile bounds

Abstract

We prove an anisotropic determinant comparison for paired rank-one differential operators. If a Hermitian background lies above a positive definite probe’s inverse plus a linear covariance term, the resulting polynomial is positive and its logarithmic matrix gradient is bounded by the probe. Both statements hold on the closed matrix inequality. The proof uses a sharp contraction inequality derived from mixed Hodge–Riemann relations and a Schur expansion that allows the update planes to overlap. Interlacing then yields a choice from the original supports of independent finite Hermitian laws. Each law varies by Hermitian operators supported on a fixed complex subspace of dimension at most two, and its support differences satisfy a determinant condition. For rectangular rank-one coefficients and arbitrary independent finite complex scalar laws, this gives a weighted spectral-norm bound and a comparison with a circular operator with the same scalar variances. With prescribed, possibly asymmetric row- and column-type variance caps, the variational bound is exactly the worst-case supremum. Finite real fair-sign examples on weighted bipartite trees approach it.

Plain-language summary

Suppose independent random choices each produce a matrix from a finite list. Can we pick one matrix from each list so that their sum is close to the sum of the means? For the Hermitian result, the difference between any two values of a given choice acts on a fixed complex subspace of dimension at most two and vanishes on its orthogonal complement. Each list may contain several distinct matrices. On a two-dimensional subspace, every pairwise difference must have nonpositive determinant when restricted to that subspace.

For rectangular contributions that are scalar multiples of fixed rank-one matrices, the bound uses how variance is distributed across row and column types. When the available information is a table of variance caps, the best universal bound is the infimum of an explicit function of one positive number per type. That bound applies to every finite instance, and finite tree examples can come as close to it as desired.

1. Matrix comparison

Notation and reading guide

All matrix spaces in §§1–4 are finite dimensional over ℂ\mathbb C. The notation X≽YX\succeq Y means X−YX-Y is positive semidefinite, and X≻YX\succ Y means it is positive definite. We use ∥⋅∥\|\cdot\| for the operator norm, λmax\lambda_{\max} for the largest Hermitian eigenvalue, and X*X^* for the adjoint. An empty determinant equals one. For the selection and profile statements, readers need only the trace pairing, positive semidefinite order, the Schur complement, and the variational characterization of the largest eigenvalue. Section 2 states the needed consequence of mixed Hodge–Riemann relations and translates it into derivatives of determinants. Section 3 isolates a single row and column of the matrix and applies that inequality to the terms involving the off-diagonal block. Section 4 passes from the expected characteristic polynomial to an original support point. Section 5 obtains the rectangular bound by Hermitian dilation and explains its circular norm through the adjacency operator of a tree. Section 6 uses a related tree to prove sharpness of each variance profile.

A tree variational principle

Take a nonempty finite set of types. A vertex of type vv has a prescribed finite list of children, each with a specified type ww and a linear edge map BB from the nonzero finite-dimensional space at type vv to the one at type ww. An edge contributes BB toward the child and B*B^* toward the parent. Let ℋv\mathcal H_v be the self-adjoint adjacency operator on the tree rooted at type vv, acting on the direct sum of the internal spaces at its vertices. Fix two finite sets of types ℒ,ℛ\mathcal L,\mathcal R and finite numbers suv≥0s_{uv}\geq0 on ordered pairs of opposite types. Proof of the profile theorem

For the upper bound use U=∑u∈ℒguPuU=\sum_{u\in\mathcal L}g_uP_u and V=∑v∈ℛgvPvV=\sum_{v\in\mathcal R}g_vP_v in (11). With no active edge both covariance maps vanish and both sides of the theorem are zero. For the lower bound, apply the tree variational principle of §5 to the internal space ℂ\mathbb C at each type vv, with one child of type ww and scalar edge map svw\sqrt{s_{vw}} whenever svw>0s_{vw}>0. At each root, density of finitely supported vectors gives the upper edge as the supremum of the largest eigenvalues of its finite root balls. Isolated types contribute edge zero.

Given a root type and depth hh, take an integer NN so large that dvw=⌊Nsvw⌋≥2d_{vw}=\lfloor N s_{vw}\rfloor\geq2 on every active directed pair. Grow a finite bipartite tree to depth hh. At a node of type vv allocate dvwd_{vw} children of type ww, subtracting one if the parent is of type ww. The root has no subtraction. Group vertices at each depth by their full type history from the root and use the normalized indicator of each nonempty class. Distinct histories give orthogonal vectors. 7. The scalar constant and related work

Proof of the scalar-background specialization

In the original rank-one positive semidefinite problem, every finite instance with positive variance has a support choice with discrepancy strictly below two standard deviations. The examples cited in §5 have minimum discrepancy arbitrarily close to two standard deviations, so two is the smallest universal constant. Normalize ∥∑iwiDi2∥=1\|\sum_i w_iD_i^2\|=1 and dilate a Hermitian rank-one family as in §5. The two diagonal blocks of 𝒦(I)\mathcal K(I) are at most the identity. At a scalar background xIxI for x≥2x\geq2 the gradient commutes with the independent scalar phase rotations of the two blocks. A maximum-gradient direction can therefore be chosen in just one block. The scalar background gives z=0z=0, the two blocks of each pair give J=0J=0, and the gradient column in (4) gives v=0v=0. Related work and scope

Kyng, Luh and Song proved a four-standard-deviation bound for independent scalar choices multiplying rank-one positive semidefinite matrices. Song and Yue give a worst-case lower bound approaching two standard deviations in that same class and develop a rank-two compression estimate. The scalar-background proof above establishes the finite strict upper bound at positive variance and uses a vanishing first-order vector. In the contraction (2), the first-order vector can be nonzero, and the pseudoinverse handles null directions directly, including for singular mixed directions. Equations (4)–(5) control the terms from overlapping pairs by one covariance map. The closed non-scalar probe region also applies to the plane laws in §4 and to rectangular coefficients in §5. The three-projector calculation in §6 illustrates the comparison of two bounds for a given instance. Kyng–Luh–Song, Theorem 1.4 · Song–Yue, Theorem 5.1

Wang, Lau and Zhou obtain a general free comparison with a parameter-dependent additive error and give an algorithm for bounded independent Hermitian supports. There is a direct comparison of free models on the subclass of centered scalar laws uniform on {1,−1,i,−i}\{1,-1,i,-i\}, followed by Hermitian dilation. On this subclass the real and imaginary parts of each scalar are uncorrelated and have variance 1/21/2. Matching them to independent semicircular variables of variance 1/21/2 gives a circular variable of variance one.
