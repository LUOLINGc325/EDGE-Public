Self-calibration from heterogeneous convolution times

26 November 2026

Abstract

We study independent observations whose distributions are a common unknown density convolved with a symmetric Lévy semigroup at known bounded deterministic times. No pure-noise observations or repeated design points are required, and the signal characteristic function may have arbitrary zeros. The centered time information I=∑i(ai−a‾)2I=\sum_i(a_i-\bar a)^2 controls internal calibration. We first prove design-uniform minimax risk laws for an unknown semigroup, an unknown Gaussian variance, and a supplied supersmooth baseline with a supplied finite-jump radius. We then resolve two further boundaries. When both the Gaussian variance and a jump measure of mass at most Λ\Lambda are unknown, the squared-L2L^2 density risk is comparable to the sum of [log(e+N)]−s[\log(e+N)]^{-s}, (1+I)−min(s,2)/2(1+I)^{-\min(s,2)/2}, and ℓ−smin{1,Λℓ}min(s,2)\ell^{-s}\min\{1,\Lambda\ell\}^{\min(s,2)}, truncated at a constant, where ℓ=log(e+IΛ2)\ell=\log(e+I\Lambda^2). The interaction term is larger than the corresponding supplied-baseline term and is attained by conservative Gaussian extrapolation combined with local calibration. Its matching lower bound uses exact Gaussian–jump compensation on the central support of a bandlimited signal. The Gaussian component itself has risk comparable to min{1,(1+I)−1+Λ2}\min\{1,(1+I)^{-1}+\Lambda^2\}; it can fail to be identifiable while the signal remains identifiable. Even on signals with strictly positive characteristic functions, pointwise identification does not imply uniform consistency. Finally, for a fixed known stable baseline of index β\beta, and fixed known signal smoothness, we determine the smallest worst-case ratio between the risk of a single radius-independent estimator and the minimax risk when the radius is supplied. This ratio is of order [log(e+log(e+log(e+N)))]2s/β[\log(e+\log(e+\log(e+N)))]^{2s/\beta}. A thresholded centered score attains it, and a constrained-risk argument proves necessity. The adaptive upper envelope also holds for a larger known-baseline class; the matching competitive lower bound is asserted for stable baselines. All rates are comparisons up to constants, not efficient leading constants or a claim of worldwide priority.

1.1. What is already known

Unknown-error deconvolution with a separate sample of pure noise has sharp minimax theory [J09]. Inverse problems with a separately observed noisy operator have general calibration and minimax theory [HR08]. Neither a second calibration term nor joint signal/operator perturbation is a new general principle. Known heteroscedastic error distributions have also been studied for arbitrary design sequences [DM08].

Meister, Stadtmüller and Wagner [MSW10] treat two independent groups with noise related by dilation and aggregation. Their fixed-parameter model and signal class differ from (1.1)–(1.5). They already show that unknown error shape can change the type of convergence rate. Garnier [G24] studies noisy compound-Poisson decompounding through independent channels. Its marginal characteristic functions have a common unknown factor and a time-dependent semigroup factor; its two-time bound already contains an inverse squared time gap. Its target is the jump density, and the common measurement-error characteristic function is assumed nonzero. Consequently, two-time ratios, time-gap conditioning, and the general phenomenon of a shape-uncertainty penalty are not claimed here as new.

Linton and Whang [LW02] use different aggregation sizes to separate individual and common components. In the inspected institutional working-paper version, the normalized aggregate has characteristic function ϕε(t)ϕX0(t/r)r\phi_\varepsilon(t)\phi_{X_0}(t/r)^r. Stable X0X_0 gives a genuine semigroup special case, including Gaussian noise; a general Lévy exponent yields rψ0(t/r)r\psi_0(t/r) rather than a fixed exponent multiplied by a known time. The inspected results use a fixed finite set of integer aggregation sizes, repeated observations within each size group, and nonvanishing assumptions. Kappus [K18] studies irregularly sampled Lévy increments, whose common initial distribution is known rather than an unknown density to be separated.

Misspecified-error robustness also has an existing literature. Meister [M04] studies the limiting integrated squared error of a specified Fourier estimator under a fixed error misspecification; the inspected institutional version gives the associated limit functional in Theorem 1. Kato, Sasaki and Ura [KSU21] construct identification-robust confidence bands from paired repeated measurements using direct moment restrictions, without a nonvanishing characteristic-function requirement. Neither robustness nor avoiding characteristic-function division is therefore claimed as a new general principle. Section 8 concerns a different target: an all-estimator minimax law uniform in a shrinking finite-jump radius and an internally informative exposure design.

The Gaussian component of an unknown Lévy law has its own identification and estimation difficulties. Matias [Ma02] studies unknown Gaussian variance under signal restrictions, while Neumann and Reiß [NR09, Remark 3.2] give a general Lévy example of nonuniform Gaussian-component estimation. Chen, Delaigle and Hall [CDH10] study unknown stable scale together with a compound-Poisson component in an increment experiment. Those results do not identify the radius-uniform interaction with an unrestricted common signal and centered exposure information in Section 9. We do not claim that Gaussian–jump separation or its instability is a new general phenomenon.

Adaptation to an unknown contamination proportion also has a substantial prior theory. Liu and Gao [LG19] study pointwise density loss, and Zhang and Ren [ZR23, Theorems 3.3–3.4] address global LpL^p loss and unknown-proportion adaptation with known smoothness under their stated conditions. Their iid mixture experiments differ from the supersmooth internally calibrated convolution experiment here. The constrained-risk principle used in Section 10 is due to Brown and Low [BL96], with later general-loss formulations such as Duchi and Ruan [DR21]; neither the principle nor its Hilbert-space consequence is claimed as new. Triple iterated logarithms already occur in minimax theory, for example in sparse high-dimensional change-point detection [LGS21]. Our claim concerns the exact cost for this radius-adaptation criterion, not the first occurrence of that mathematical expression.

The contribution is a design-uniform classification within the specified common-signal experiment, including Fourier zeros and matching alternatives inside the positive Lévy cone. Section 8 establishes the supplied-baseline, supplied-radius law. Sections 9 and 10 complete two further statistical boundaries: simultaneous Gaussian-scale and shrinking-jump uncertainty creates a new interaction risk and an exact component-identification distinction; withholding the jump radius in the known-baseline experiment incurs an unavoidable, attainable worst-case oracle ratio. The latter is a global comparison over designs and radii, not a claim that the proposed adaptive envelope is pointwise minimax for every design-radius pair. Its estimator may use the fixed smoothness ss, whereas the supplied-radius estimators do not need ss. These are specified missing boundaries inferred from inspected results, not problems attributed to another author as an explicitly stated conjecture. The literature review is bounded and does not certify worldwide priority. Version and access limitations, including the unavailable final typeset version of [LW02], remain as stated in the references.

7. Interpretation and boundaries

The design enters the unknown-semigroup lower bound through centered times because a common amount of convolution can be moved between the signal and the noise. Moving the two legal jump components at time a‾\bar a cancels their average effect. Only the deviations ai−a‾a_i-\bar a reveal which component was assigned to the signal. The upper score uses the same centering, but its proof does not assume that the least-favorable family describes a typical signal.

The information law concerns the common signal, not estimation of every nuisance frequency. A concrete distinction is possible: when the fixed class bounds include the density η\eta, take f=ηf=\eta and the two noise exponents (4.14). The signal characteristic function is supported on [−4,4][-4,4], while the difference of the exponents is supported on ℬT\mathcal B_T, disjoint from that interval. All observation characteristic functions then agree under the two different exponents, for every design. The common density can still be identified when I>0I\gt 0 by Proposition 2.4. This example does not assert that every parameter class has this particular nuisance alias; it illustrates why full nuisance recovery is unnecessary.

There are several explicit limits to the results. The exposure interval is fixed and bounded away from zero. If it expands or approaches zero with NN, a scalar INI_N need not summarize the relevant error amplification. Ordinary-smooth envelopes are not covered: there, constants in an exponential amplification bound become rate exponents and cannot be discarded as they are in a logarithmic rate. The noise is symmetric, so its exponent is real; an unknown drift or general asymmetric phase requires additional work. The procedure knows β\beta and an envelope constant; it does not adapt to an unknown envelope. No multidimensional or adaptive-design theorem is asserted.

Theorem 2.3 holds for each fixed positive jump-intensity allowance. Its constants may deteriorate as that allowance tends to zero. Section 8 supplies an additional uniform analysis for a known baseline and a supplied shrinking radius. Section 9 resolves joint uncertainty in an unknown Gaussian variance and a supplied shrinking jump allowance. Section 10 resolves the global cost of unknown-radius adaptation for a fixed known stable baseline. Unknown-radius adaptation in the joint Gaussian–jump model, and a matching adaptive lower bound for every baseline in (8.1), remain outside the results. All rate comparisons are up to fixed multiplicative constants; no efficient constants or finite-sample optimal designs beyond the elementary information bound are established.

The numerical materials accompanying this manuscript check formulas, positivity, selected integrals, and finite designs. They cannot establish the uniform minimax quantifiers. Those quantifiers rest on the analytical proofs in Sections 3–6 and 8–10. The novelty claim is restricted to the exact combination of model, parameter classes, design uniformity, and comparison theorems stated here; it is subject to the documented literature limits and future external review.

10.5. Scope of the adaptation result

The matching loss occurs along designs whose information tends to infinity while the exposure range remains fixed and the two exposure groups approach one another. It is therefore distinct from exact nonidentification at equal exposures. At equal exposures, the finite-sample bound (10.41) still applies, but the known-radius bias term itself is of order Λ2\Lambda^2.

The threshold in (10.9) protects the small risk available at the exact known baseline. The lower construction shows why that protection has a cost on nearby, weakly distinguished convolution alternatives. The constrained-risk method is inherited from prior work [BL96, DR21]; the specific centered-design transfer, radius comparison, and matching simultaneous factor concern the experiment defined here.

The entire baseline remains known in this section. The estimator may use the fixed smoothness ss to choose KK, and no simultaneous adaptation to unknown ss or β\beta is asserted. The two-sided factor (10.6) is established for known stable baselines; (10.4) is the separate baseline-uniform upper statement. Neither theorem provides exact leading constants or a pointwise constrained-minimax characterization of every design and radius.
