Good-Turing is asymptotically minimax for missing-mass estimation

26 November 2026

Abstract

The missing mass of a sample from a discrete distribution is the total probability of symbols absent from that sample. The Good-Turing estimator, the observed singleton count divided by the sample size, has worst-case squared risk asymptotic to C/nC/n, where C=0.6080367865…C=0.6080367865\ldots. The bounds established by Acharya, Bao, Kang and Sun in 2018 have the smaller lower-bound constant 0.570077…0.570077\ldots, obtained from uniform distributions with unknown support. We prove that the Good-Turing constant is the exact asymptotic minimax constant over all discrete distributions. If w=W(2)w=W(2), then

limn→∞ninfM̂supp𝔼p(M̂−Mn(p))2=w2+w24. \lim_{n\to\infty} n\inf_{\widehat M}\sup_p \mathbb E_p(\widehat M-M_n(p))^2 =\frac w2+\frac{w^2}{4}.

The lower bound uses a normalized Poisson-mixture experiment and a direct Bayesian information inequality for a target depending on both latent intensities and observed counts. A correction by increments of the Good-Turing estimation error transfers the lower bound to every deterministic sample size. The argument explains why efficiency within the uniform subclass does not determine the unrestricted minimax constant. The Poisson-mixture efficiency principle is classical; the proof isolates its normalization and fixed-sample transfer. We also give a self-contained proof of the known sharp upper bound.

1. Introduction and scope

Let Y1,…,YnY_1,\ldots,Y_n be independent observations from an unknown probability distribution p=(pi)i≥1p=(p_i)_{i\ge1} on the positive integers. The sample reveals only a finite collection of symbols. The conditional probability that the next observation is a new symbol is the missing mass

Mn(p)=∑i≥1pi𝟏{Ni(n)=0},Ni(n)=∑j=1n𝟏{Yj=i}. M_n(p)=\sum_{i\ge1}p_i\mathbf1\{N_i(n)=0\}, \qquad N_i(n)=\sum_{j=1}^n\mathbf1\{Y_j=i\}. \tag{1.1}

This target is random: it depends on the same sample used to estimate it. For an estimator δn\delta_n, define

Rn(δn,p)=𝔼p{δn(Y1,…,Yn)−Mn(p)}2,Rn*=infδnsupp∈𝒫Rn(δn,p), R_n(\delta_n,p)=\mathbb E_p\{\delta_n(Y_1,\ldots,Y_n)-M_n(p)\}^2, \qquad R_n^*=\inf_{\delta_n}\sup_{p\in\mathcal P}R_n(\delta_n,p), \tag{1.2}

where 𝒫\mathcal P is the class of all probability distributions on the positive integers. Estimators may be randomized, although conditional expectation over their extra randomization cannot increase squared risk. Clipping to [0,1][0,1] cannot increase risk either.

Write K1(n)=∑i𝟏{Ni(n)=1}K_1(n)=\sum_i\mathbf1\{N_i(n)=1\}. The Good-Turing estimator is

Gn=K1(n)n. G_n=\frac{K_1(n)}n. \tag{1.3}

Rajaraman, Thangaraj and Suresh [RTS17] established explicit upper and lower bounds for this decision problem. Acharya, Bao, Kang and Sun [ABKS18] identified the sharp worst-case leading constant of GnG_n and the different minimax constant for the subclass of uniform laws. Their author working paper asks whether the uniform-subclass constant is also the unrestricted minimax constant [ABKS17]. The two quantities must be distinguished: a distribution maximizing the risk of one estimator need not be least favorable when a restricted model is known.

The efficiency of estimating sums involving observable and latent random variables was developed by Zhang [Z05]. In particular, Robbins’s u,vu,v estimators are efficient in broad Poisson-mixture models. That result concerns a different experiment from (1.2). The present proof uses the exact normalized latent ratio and a deterministic-sample comparison. The final lower bound applies to every estimator, without a local regularity restriction on the estimator.

Painsky [P23] develops generalized Good-Turing procedures and improved risk bounds. Its bibliographic record, abstract, and author-provided software were available for comparison; its full text was not obtained. The comparison below therefore concerns one documented estimator and does not purport to survey all results in that article. This is a limitation of the literature comparison; the theorem is proved directly in Sections 3-6. The mathematical theorem below does not assert that the finite-sample Good-Turing estimator is minimax, admissible, or best for every distribution. It concerns the leading constant of the unrestricted worst-case squared risk. It does not concern multiplicative loss, estimation of expected missing mass as a deterministic parameter, or full-distribution estimation.

Compatibility with a documented generalized Good-Turing rule. The author-provided software [P23S] and the explicit formula in [PGKL26, Equation (2)] give the rule

GnGGT=(1n−2.08n1.7)K1(n)+4.1n1.7K2(n),K2(n)=∑i𝟏{Ni(n)=2}. G_n^{\mathrm{GGT}} =\left(\frac1n-\frac{2.08}{n^{1.7}}\right)K_1(n) +\frac{4.1}{n^{1.7}}K_2(n), \qquad K_2(n)=\sum_i\mathbf1\{N_i(n)=2\}. \tag{1.4}

Since K1(n)≤nK_1(n)\le n and K2(n)≤n/2K_2(n)\le n/2, deterministically

|GnGGT−Gn|≤4.13n−0.7=o(n−1/2). |G_n^{\mathrm{GGT}}-G_n| \le4.13\,n^{-0.7}=o(n^{-1/2}).

Writing dn=4.13n−0.7d_n=4.13\,n^{-0.7}, the uniform bound suppRn(Gn,p)=O(n−1)\sup_p R_n(G_n,p)=O(n^{-1}) from Section 3 implies

supp|Rn(GnGGT,p)−Rn(Gn,p)|≤2dnsuppRn(Gn,p)+dn2=o(n−1). \sup_p\bigl|R_n(G_n^{\mathrm{GGT}},p)-R_n(G_n,p)\bigr| \le2d_n\sqrt{\sup_p R_n(G_n,p)}+d_n^2 =o(n^{-1}).

Thus this rule has the same first-order worst-case risk constant as Good-Turing. This observation verifies compatibility of a specific documented estimator with Theorem 2.1; it is not a new contribution or a claim to have examined the full text of [P23].

2. Main theorem

Define

F(c)=(1+c)e−c−e−2c,c>0,C*=supc>0F(c). F(c)=(1+c)e^{-c}-e^{-2c},\qquad c>0, \quad C_* =\sup_{c>0}F(c). \tag{2.1}

Theorem 2.1 (sharp asymptotic minimax constant). For the experiment and loss in (1.1)-(1.2),

limn→∞nRn*=C*=limn→∞nsupp∈𝒫Rn(Gn,p). \lim_{n\to\infty}nR_n^*=C_* =\lim_{n\to\infty} n\sup_{p\in\mathcal P}R_n(G_n,p). \tag{2.2}

If ww is the unique positive solution of wexp(w)=2w\exp(w)=2, then

C*=w2+w24=0.6080367865228819614…,w=0.8526055020137254913…. C_*=\frac w2+\frac{w^2}4 =0.6080367865228819614\ldots, \qquad w=0.8526055020137254913\ldots. \tag{2.3}

The proof has four components. Section 3 establishes the known upper bound uniformly over countable alphabets. Section 4 gives a Bayesian lower bound for a normalized Poisson-mixture target. Section 5 proves continuity, in mean square, of the Good-Turing estimation error when the sample size changes by a sublinear amount. Section 6 uses that continuity to transfer the lower bound to arbitrary deterministic sample sizes.

3. The sharp Good-Turing upper bound

We include the argument to make the asymptotic comparison self-contained. The sharp constant in Proposition 3.3 is the established result of [ABKS17, Theorem 4; ABKS18], with the earlier risk analysis from [RTS17]; it is not a new contribution here. All constants in this section are absolute and all error bounds are uniform over pp.

Lemma 3.1 (elementary exponential approximations). For fixed r∈{0,1,2}r\in\{0,1,2\} and all sufficiently large nn,

sup0≤x≤1|(1−x)n−r−e−nx|≤Crn,sup0≤x≤1x|(1−x)n−e−nx|≤Cn2. \sup_{0\le x\le1}|(1-x)^{n-r}-e^{-nx}|\le\frac{C_r}{n}, \qquad \sup_{0\le x\le1}x|(1-x)^n-e^{-nx}|\le\frac C{n^2}. \tag{3.1}

Also sup0≤x≤1x2(1−x)n−2≤C/n2\sup_{0\le x\le1}x^2(1-x)^{n-2}\le C/n^2.

Proof. For x≤1/2x\le1/2, use

For fixed rr and n≥2r+2n\ge2r+2, the difference in the first inequality is bounded by Cr(x+nx2)exp(−nx/2)C_r(x+nx^2)\exp(-nx/2). Maximizing each monomial after writing z=nxz=nx proves its bound. For x≥1/2x\ge1/2, the two exponential terms are exponentially small in nn, and the same inequalities follow after enlarging the constants. Lemma 3.2 (uniform risk expansion). As n→∞n\to\infty,

nRn(Gn,p)=∑ipi(1+npi)e−npi−(∑ipie−npi)2+O(n−1), nR_n(G_n,p) =\sum_i p_i(1+np_i)e^{-np_i} -\left(\sum_i p_i e^{-np_i}\right)^2+O(n^{-1}), \tag{3.2}

Proof. Direct multinomial counting gives, for n≥4n\ge4,

For example, the two off-diagonal contributions from 𝔼Gn2\mathbb E G_n^2 and 𝔼Mn2\mathbb E M_n^2, together with the cross term, have coefficient

n−1n+(1−pi−pj)2−2(1−pi−pj)=(pi+pj)2−1n. \frac{n-1}{n}+(1-p_i-p_j)^2-2(1-p_i-p_j) =(p_i+p_j)^2-\frac1n.

By Lemma 3.1, the last line of (3.3) is O(n−2)O(n^{-2}). Replacing the power terms in the first and third lines by exponentials costs O(n−2)O(n^{-2}), using ∑ipi=1\sum_i p_i=1 and ∑i≠jpipj≤1\sum_{i\ne j}p_ip_j\le1. Finally, completing the off-diagonal exponential sum to a square changes the risk by at most

Proposition 3.3 (known sharp upper constant). limn→∞nsuppRn(Gn,p)=C*\lim_{n\to\infty}n\sup_p R_n(G_n,p)=C_*.

Proof. Let Λ\Lambda take the value npinp_i with probability pip_i, and put Z=exp(−Λ)Z=\exp(-\Lambda). The right side of (3.2), without its remainder, is

𝔼[Z(1−logZ)]−(𝔼Z)2. \mathbb E[Z(1-\log Z)]-(\mathbb EZ)^2.

The function z(1−logz)z(1-\log z) is concave on (0,1](0,1] and extends continuously at zero. Jensen’s inequality bounds this expression by

In particular, Rn*=O(1/n)R_n^*=O(1/n) and limsupn→∞nRn*≤C*\limsup_{n\to\infty}nR_n^*\le C_*.

5. Changing the sample size

This section controls the estimation error itself, rather than the change in the missing mass. The latter change may be too large at the root-nn scale.

7. Interpretation and limitations

The lower bound is obtained from arbitrarily narrow, but nondegenerate, mixtures of intensities near the uniform point. For each fixed mixture width, the available local mixing directions are rich. Only after taking the sample-size limit and the supremum over finite direction collections do we shrink that width. Setting the width to zero at the outset produces the easier uniform model and loses these directions. The order of these operations is essential.

Theorem 2.1 identifies a global worst-case leading constant. It leaves room for finite-sample improvements, distribution-specific improvements, and substantial gains when prior structural information about pp is available. It does not say that every near-uniform law is asymptotically least favorable under every local formulation. The mixing priors are a lower-bound construction, not an assumption required by the Good-Turing procedure.

Three ingredients in the proof have separate roles. The normalized ratio in (4.2) enforces total probability one exactly. The generalized Bayesian inequality in Lemma 4.1 deals with a sample-dependent target and applies to arbitrary estimators. The increment correction in (6.6) proves a lower bound along every deterministic sample size, rather than merely ruling out an eventually uniform improvement. The classical Poisson-mixture u,vu,v principle [Z05] motivates the first-order utility, but does not by itself supply these last two transitions in the form needed for (1.2).

The comparison with generalized Good-Turing procedures is restricted to the explicit rule discussed in Section 1. No assertion is made here about every finite-sample refinement or every estimator in [P23].
