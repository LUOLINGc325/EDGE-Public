# Fractional neural kernels: radial spectral laws, threshold interference, and inverse limits

## Abstract

We study random-feature kernels on a probability sphere generated by finite sums of shifted positive-part powers. For Gaussian weights we determine exact harmonic and ranked spectral constants, classify polynomial parity cancellations, and resolve Conjecture 1 of Schmutz et al. (2025). We then prove a radial-distribution structure theorem under only the natural activation moment condition. The nonatomic radial part contributes deterministic parity amplitudes in mean square, even for singular continuous laws; each radial atom retains an oscillatory amplitude. The ranked Weyl constant is the Haar average of a fractional power of the resulting continuous function on the compact group generated by all atomic phases. This law remains valid when no harmonic-parity limit exists and yields explicit arithmetic discontinuities for a single radial shell. At inverse-degree threshold scaling, we prove compact-uniform Gram limits for arbitrary radial measures and show that their two parity families determine the radial law exactly. The proof combines classical fractional Jacobi estimates, Wiener averaging, and compact-group equidistribution. The new structure is the radial decomposition of threshold spectra and their inverse spectral realization, rather than new special-function or transform-inversion methods.

## 1. Model, notation, and relation to prior work

Fix an integer \(d\ge2\), a real number \(p\ge0\), and a finite number of thresholds. Surface measure \(\sigma\) on \(S^{d-1}\subset\mathbb R^d\) is normalized to mass one. All coefficients and thresholds are real. Write

\[
\phi(t)=P(t)+\sum_{j=1}^J a_j(t+c_j)_+^p,\qquad
K_\phi(x,y)=\mathbb E_{w\sim N(0,I_d)}[\phi(w\cdot x)\phi(w\cdot y)]. \tag{1}
\]

Here \(P\) is a fixed polynomial. For \(p=0\), \(t_+^0\) means \(\mathbf1_{\{t>0\}}\); its value at zero does not affect any assertion. Equal thresholds are combined, with aggregate coefficient \(a_c\); missing coefficients are set to zero. The resulting finite set is completed by reflection \(c\mapsto-c\). We allow all aggregate coefficients to vanish. The zero polynomial has degree \(-\infty\).

The integral operator with kernel (1) acts on \(L^2(\sigma)\). Its eigenvalue on the space of degree-\(\ell\) spherical harmonics is denoted \(\mu_\ell\). The positive eigenvalues, repeated with their multiplicities and arranged in nonincreasing order, are \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\ldots\), whenever the operator has infinite rank. An infinite-dimensional zero eigenspace is recorded separately; zeros are not inserted before positive eigenvalues in this ordering. We use \(u_n\sim v_n\) only for a ratio tending to one.

Set

\[
\begin{split}
\alpha&=(d-2)/2,&\beta&=(d-3)/2,&q&=p+\alpha,\\
\Lambda&=p+d/2=q+1,&s&=d+2p=2\Lambda,&\kappa&=(d-1)/s,\\
H&=\frac{2^{(d-2)/2}\Gamma(d/2)\Gamma(p+1)}{\pi},&
m&=\frac{2^p\Gamma(p+d/2)}{\Gamma(d/2)},&
A&=\frac{H^2m}{2}
=\frac{2^{d+p-3}\Gamma(d/2)\Gamma(p+1)^2\Gamma(p+d/2)}{\pi^2}.
\end{split} \tag{2}
\]

Thus \(0\lt \kappa\lt 1\). Dimension, power, number of terms, and coefficients are fixed throughout every asymptotic statement. The uniformity below concerns compact sets of the rescaled thresholds only.

Schmutz et al. [1, equations (9)–(12), Conjecture 1] ask for a positive finite ranked spectral limit for the single term \((t+c)_+^p\), under exactly the Gaussian-weight and probability-sphere conventions in (1). Their symbol for asymptotic comparison is explicitly defined as a ratio with a positive finite limit, a stronger assertion than two-sided order bounds. Their interpretation of the decay exponent and the resulting ambiguity between activation regularity and latent dimension are prior results and motivations, not new statistical identifiability claims here.

The harmonic method and the relation between singularities and spectra are established tools. Bach [2] treats homogeneous integer powers and their parity structure. Bietti and Bach [3, Theorem 7] transfer endpoint power singularities of fixed zonal kernels into harmonic asymptotics. Xie et al. [4, Appendix 3.3, equations A32 and A34] use Gaussian radial averaging and Rodrigues formulas for a deterministic-threshold ReLU model. Bordelon and Pehlevan [5, Appendix, “Nonlinear simple cells”] discuss the threshold-independent power of the spectral tail in a circular Gabor model. None of these ingredients is claimed as new.

Li et al. [6, Theorems 8 and 10] transfer known spherical eigenvalue orders to more general domains; those order results do not supply the moving-threshold limits considered here. Holzmüller and Schölpple [7, Assumption 4 and Remarks 8 and A.7] analyze activations with their only nonsmooth point at zero, and discuss extensions to other locations. Liu and Wang [8, equation (2.7), Theorems 2.1–2.2] already provide fractional Gegenbauer identities, oscillatory constants, and weighted endpoint estimates. We give a convenient self-contained estimate for the needed cap integral, while crediting that established special-function analysis. The exact Ferrers and Jacobi representations used below are classical [9].

Tsuchida, Roosta, and Gallagher [10, Proposition 1] already establish zero-bias ReLU kernel invariance for rotationally invariant weight densities with finite second moment. More generally, homogeneity makes the zero-bias radial dependence a single moment. Thus neither the use of non-Gaussian weights nor this homogeneous invariance is new. Wiener averaging and equidistribution are classical [11,12]; we give the exact elementary forms needed below. The fixed-bias envelope is also a Williamson-type integral transform: at positive integer orders its inversion belongs to established theory [13, Proposition 3.1], not to the novelty claimed here.

The Gaussian results provide a complete solution of the specified source conjecture and an exact finite-threshold interference law. The further contribution is a structure theorem for arbitrary radial measures, including singular continuous and countably atomic laws: it identifies which oscillations disappear in density, which survive as compact-group amplitudes, and how those amplitudes determine the ranked spectral constant. The inverse-degree transition family additionally identifies the radial measure that zero-bias homogeneous kernels cannot distinguish. These are infinite-width population-kernel results. They do not assert finite-width concentration, finite-sample identification rates or stability, nonuniform-input laws, or corresponding theorems for deep networks. The literature comparison is bounded by the identified sources and does not establish worldwide priority.

## 2. Main results

### Theorem 1. Fixed-threshold interference and exact degeneracy

For the activation (1), define

\[
S=\sum_c a_c^2e^{-c^2/2},\qquad
T=\sum_c a_ca_{-c}e^{-c^2/2}. \tag{3}
\]

The sums run over the finite reflection-completed threshold set, so that the zero threshold occurs once and a nonzero opposite pair occurs twice in \(T\). For \(\varepsilon\in\{+1,-1\}\), put

\[
b_\varepsilon=A\{S-\varepsilon\cos(\pi p)T\}. \tag{4}
\]

Then, separately along \((-1)^\ell=\varepsilon\),

\[
\ell^s\mu_\ell\longrightarrow b_\varepsilon. \tag{5}
\]

The following alternatives are exhaustive.

1. If \(S=0\), the activation is the polynomial \(P\), and \(\mu_\ell=0\) for every \(\ell>\deg P\).
2. If \(S>0\) and \(p\notin\mathbb Z\), both constants in (4) are strictly positive.
3. If \(S>0\) and \(p\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\), a branch \(b_\varepsilon\) vanishes if and only if
   \[
   a_{-c}=\eta a_c\quad\text{for every }c,\qquad
   \eta=\varepsilon(-1)^p. \tag{6}
   \]
   In that event the entire branch is exactly zero for \(\ell>\max(p,\deg P)\) of parity \(\varepsilon\). The other branch has constant \(2AS>0\).

In particular, a vanishing leading branch in this class is never merely a positive branch with an unspecified faster tail.

### Theorem 2. Ranked positive spectrum

If \(S>0\), the positive spectrum is infinite. Define

\[
D=\frac{b_+^\kappa+b_-^\kappa}{(d-1)!},\qquad 0^\kappa=0. \tag{7}
\]

For the number \(N(t)\) of positive eigenvalues strictly larger than \(t>0\), counted with multiplicity,

\[
N(t)\sim D t^{-\kappa}\quad(t\downarrow0),\qquad
\lambda_n\sim D^{1/\kappa}n^{-1/\kappa}\quad(n\to\infty). \tag{8}
\]

The finite exceptional degrees, including all changes caused by \(P\), do not change these constants.

### Theorem 3. Compact-uniform coalescing-threshold limit

Fix \(J\), the vector \(a=(a_1,\ldots,a_J)^\top\), and \(P\). In (1) replace \(c_j\) by \(\tau_j/\ell\), and let \(\mu_\ell(\tau/\ell)\) be the degree-\(\ell\) eigenvalue of this degree-dependent kernel. Repeated components of \(\tau\) are allowed. For \(R_s\) with chi density of real parameter \(s=d+2p\), define

\[
G^{(\varepsilon)}_{ij}(\tau)
=\mathbb E\left[
\cos\frac{\tau_i-\tau_j}{R_s}
-\varepsilon\cos\left(\frac{\tau_i+\tau_j}{R_s}-\pi p\right)
\right]. \tag{9}
\]

For every compact \(B\subset\mathbb R^J\),

\[
\lim_{\substack{\ell\to\infty\\(-1)^\ell=\varepsilon}}
\sup_{\tau\in B}
\left|\ell^s\mu_\ell(\tau/\ell)-A a^\top G^{(\varepsilon)}(\tau)a\right|=0. \tag{10}
\]

The matrix is continuous in \(\tau\) and positive semidefinite. More precisely, with

\[
\gamma_+=(p+1)\pi/2,\qquad \gamma_-=p\pi/2,
\]

it has the Gram representation

\[
G^{(\varepsilon)}_{ij}(\tau)
=2\mathbb E\left[
\cos(\tau_i/R_s-\gamma_\varepsilon)
\cos(\tau_j/R_s-\gamma_\varepsilon)
\right]. \tag{11}
\]

The statement is a joint limit of a family of kernels, not an assertion about ranking the eigenvalues of one changing kernel uniformly in its whole spectrum.

### Corollary 4. A single threshold and noncommuting limits

For \(\phi(t)=(t+c)_+^p\), the harmonic constants are

\[
\lim_{\substack{\ell\to\infty\\(-1)^\ell=\varepsilon}}\ell^s\mu_\ell(c)
=\begin{cases}
Ae^{-c^2/2},&c\ne0,\\
A[1-\varepsilon\cos(\pi p)],&c=0.
\end{cases} \tag{12}
\]

Consequently the ranked constant \(W(c)=\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1/\kappa}\lambda_n(c)\) exists, is positive, and equals

\[
W(c)=
\begin{cases}
Ae^{-c^2/2}\left[\dfrac{2}{(d-1)!}\right]^{1/\kappa},&c\ne0,\\[4pt]
A\left[\dfrac{(1-\cos\pi p)^\kappa+(1+\cos\pi p)^\kappa}{(d-1)!}\right]^{1/\kappa},&c=0.
\end{cases} \tag{13}
\]

This gives the exponent \(1/\kappa=1+(2p+1)/(d-1)\) with a positive finite constant for every parameter in [1, Conjecture 1]. For integer \(p\), the zero-bias branch with \(\ell\equiv p\pmod2\) is exactly zero above degree \(p\).

For every fixed \(n\), \(\lambda_n(c)\to\lambda_n(0)\) as \(c\to0\). Nevertheless,

\[
\lim_{c\to0,\ c\ne0}\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1/\kappa}\lambda_n(c)
\;>\;
\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{c\to0}n^{1/\kappa}\lambda_n(c) \tag{14}
\]

unless \(p\in\{1/2,3/2,5/2,\ldots\}\), in which case the two constants agree. For integer \(p\), the ratio of the left constant to the right constant is \(2^{1/\kappa-1}\). At the transition scale,

\[
\ell^s\mu_\ell(\tau/\ell)\longrightarrow
A\left[1-\varepsilon\mathbb E\cos\left(2\tau/R_s-\pi p\right)\right]. \tag{15}
\]

As \(\tau\to0\), (15) has the zero-bias parity limit. As \(|\tau|\to\infty\), its right side tends to \(A\). These last limits concern the explicit transition function; no uniformity for unbounded \(\tau\) is asserted in (10).

## 3. Harmonic reduction and exact ridge representation

### Lemma 5. Normalized harmonic reduction

Let

\[
\rho_d(t)=\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{\sqrt\pi\Gamma((d-1)/2)}(1-t^2)^\beta,
\quad -1\lt t\lt 1,
\]

and let \(Z_\ell(t)\) be the Jacobi polynomial \(P_\ell^{(\beta,\beta)}(t)\) divided by its value at one. For \(d=2\), this is \(T_\ell(t)\). For \(r>0\) define

\[
h_\ell(r,c)=\int_{-1}^1(rt+c)_+^p Z_\ell(t)\rho_d(t)\,dt. \tag{16}
\]

If \(R_d\sim\chi_d\), then for \(\ell>\deg P\),

\[
\mu_\ell=\mathbb E\left[\left(\sum_j a_jh_\ell(R_d,c_j)\right)^2\right]. \tag{17}
\]

The operator in (1) is positive and trace class. Its harmonic multiplicities are

\[
M_{\ell,d}=\frac{(2\ell+d-2)(\ell+d-3)!}{\ell!(d-2)!}\quad(d\ge3,\ \ell\ge1), \tag{18}
\]

with \(M_{0,d}=1\); for \(d=2\), \(M_{\ell,2}=2\) for \(\ell\ge1\).

**Proof.** The factorization \(w=R_du\) has \(u\sim\sigma\), independent of \(R_d\), whose density is

\[
f_d(r)=\frac{r^{d-1}e^{-r^2/2}}{2^{d/2-1}\Gamma(d/2)},\quad r>0. \tag{19}
\]

For a real orthonormal harmonic basis \(Y_{\ell m}\), the normalized addition formula is
\(\sum_mY_{\ell m}(x)Y_{\ell m}(u)=M_{\ell,d}Z_\ell(x\cdot u)\).
Funk–Hecke therefore gives

\[
\int\phi(ru\cdot x)Y_{\ell m}(x)d\sigma(x)
=\left(\int_{-1}^1\phi(rt)Z_\ell(t)\rho_d(t)dt\right)Y_{\ell m}(u).
\]

The polynomial contributes zero above its degree by orthogonality. Multiplication of two such coefficients and integration over \(u\) gives (17), with no additional multiplicity factor. These formulas include the usual cosine and sine basis on the circle. Formula (18) follows from the difference between the dimensions of homogeneous polynomials of degrees \(\ell\) and \(\ell-2\).

For completeness, the feature map \(Ff(w)=\int\phi(w\cdot x)f(x)d\sigma(x)\) is Hilbert–Schmidt because
\(\|F\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2=\int\mathbb E\phi(w\cdot x)^2d\sigma(x)=\mathbb E\phi(Z)^2\lt \infty\), where \(Z\sim N(0,1)\). Gaussian moments dominate the polynomial growth, including the bounded step case. The kernel operator is \(F^*F\), hence positive and trace class. This also justifies the coefficient integrals and exchanges above. \(\square\)

### Lemma 6. Exact cap integral

If \(|c|\lt r\), put \(a=-c/r=\cos\theta\), \(0\lt \theta\lt \pi\), and \(k=\ell+\alpha\). Then

\[
h_\ell(r,c)=\frac{H}{\Gamma(q+1)}r^p I_q(\theta,k),\qquad
I_q(\theta,k)=\int_0^\theta(\cos t-\cos\theta)^q\cos(kt)dt. \tag{20}
\]

**Proof.** We give the constants to specify the normalization. Set \(b=(1-a)/2\). The hypergeometric representation of the normalized Jacobi polynomial is

\[
Z_\ell(1-2z)={}_2F_1(-\ell,\ell+2\beta+1;\beta+1;z).
\]

The substitution \(t=1-2z\) in (16), followed by Euler's hypergeometric transformation, yields

\[
\begin{split}
r^{-p}h_\ell(r,c)
={}&\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{\sqrt\pi\Gamma(\beta+1)}2^{p+2\beta+1}
\int_0^b(b-z)^pz^\beta
{}_2F_1(-\ell-\beta,\ell+\beta+1;\beta+1;z)dz\\
={}&\frac{2^{p+2\beta+1}\Gamma(d/2)\Gamma(p+1)}{\sqrt\pi\Gamma(p+\beta+2)}
b^{p+\beta+1}
{}_2F_1(-\ell-\beta,\ell+\beta+1;p+\beta+2;b).
\end{split} \tag{21}
\]

Termwise beta integration is valid for \(b\lt 1\), first on every compact subinterval of \([0,1)\), and then at each such \(b\). Its assumptions hold since \(\beta\ge-1/2\) and \(p\ge0\). Put \(\zeta=p+\beta+1=q+1/2\). The Ferrers representation [9, equation 14.3.1] turns (21) into

\[
h_\ell(r,c)
=\frac{2^\beta\Gamma(d/2)\Gamma(p+1)}{\sqrt\pi}
r^p(1-a^2)^{\zeta/2}\mathsf P_{\ell+\beta}^{-\zeta}(a). \tag{22}
\]

Mehler–Dirichlet [9, equation 14.12.1], valid because \(\zeta\ge1/2\), is

\[
\mathsf P_{\ell+\beta}^{-\zeta}(\cos\theta)
=\frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}(\sin\theta)^{-\zeta}}{\Gamma(\zeta+1/2)}
\int_0^\theta(\cos t-\cos\theta)^{\zeta-1/2}\cos((\ell+\beta+1/2)t)dt.
\]

The sine powers cancel and give (20). This representation is consistent with the fractional Gegenbauer formula in [8, equation (2.7)]. No limiting argument in dimension is needed at \(d=2\): the Jacobi parameter is \(-1/2\), which is within the formulas used. \(\square\)

## 4. Uniform control of a ridge coefficient

### Lemma 7. Uniform cap estimate and interior asymptotic

For fixed \(q\ge0\), there is \(C_q\lt \infty\) such that, for \(k\ge1\) and \(0\le\theta\le\pi\),

\[
|I_q(\theta,k)|\le C_q k^{-q-1}. \tag{23}
\]

For each \(\delta>0\), uniformly on \(\delta\le\theta\le\pi-\delta\),

\[
I_q(\theta,k)
=\Gamma(q+1)(\sin\theta)^q k^{-q-1}
\cos\left(k\theta-\frac{\pi(q+1)}2\right)
+O_{q,\delta}(k^{-q-2}). \tag{24}
\]

**Proof.** For \(q=0\), \(I_0=\sin(k\theta)/k\), proving both statements. Suppose \(q>0\). Extend
\(f_\theta(t)=(\cos t-\cos\theta)^q\) evenly on \((-\theta,\theta)\) and by zero outside. Write \(u=\theta-|t|\). In the interior,

\[
|f_\theta^{(j)}(t)|\le C_{q,j}u^{q-j},\qquad j=0,1,2,\ldots, \tag{25}
\]

with constants independent of \(\theta\). To see this, factor
\(\cos t-\cos\theta=2\sin((\theta+t)/2)\sin((\theta-t)/2)\).
Each sine argument is at distance at least \(u/2\) from the endpoints \(0,\pi\). The logarithmic derivatives of each factor of order \(j\ge1\) are bounded by \(C_ju^{-j}\). Also \(0\le\cos t-\cos\theta\le u\), by the Lipschitz bound for cosine. Differentiating the exponential of \(q\) times the logarithm proves (25). At \(t=0\) the original function is smooth and even, so the two descriptions join smoothly.

If \(\theta\le4/k\), direct integration gives (23). Otherwise choose a smooth cutoff that deletes endpoint collars of width \(1/k\), is one at distance at least \(2/k\) from the endpoints, and has its \(j\)-th derivative bounded by \(C_jk^j\). The deleted integral is \(O(k^{-q-1})\) by (25) with \(j=0\). On the retained part integrate the full Fourier integral \(\int f_\theta(t)e^{ikt}dt\) by parts an integer \(L>q+1\) times. There are no boundary terms. Its differentiated amplitude has integral bounded by
\(C\int_{1/k}^{\theta}u^{q-L}du+Ck^{L-q-1}\le C'k^{L-q-1}\).
Multiplication by \(k^{-L}\), and division by two to recover the cosine integral, gives (23).

For (24), use smooth endpoint cutoffs of fixed width, uniform over the specified compact interval of \(\theta\). At the right endpoint, with \(u=\theta-t\),

\[
f_\theta(\theta-u)=u^q B_\theta(u),\qquad
B_\theta(0)=(\sin\theta)^q,
\]

where the local amplitudes and all their derivatives are uniformly bounded. The standard endpoint integral can be verified directly as follows. For a smooth cutoff amplitude \(B\), subtract \(B(0)e^{-u}\) from \(B(u)\) times its cutoff. The difference vanishes to order one at zero and has integrable, rapidly decreasing derivatives away from zero. Applying the collar and integration-by-parts estimate just used, now with exponent \(q+1\), gives an error \(O(k^{-q-2})\). The subtracted integral is exactly
\(B(0)\Gamma(q+1)(1+ik)^{-q-1}\), whose leading term is
\(B(0)\Gamma(q+1)e^{-i\pi(q+1)/2}k^{-q-1}\).
The left endpoint is its conjugate after multiplication by \(e^{ik\theta}\); the remaining smooth compact interior has arbitrarily fast Fourier decay. Taking half the real full Fourier integral proves (24) and its stated uniformity. \(\square\)

### Lemma 8. Exterior thresholds and a global integrable bound

Let \(\ell>p\). If \(c\le-r\), then \(h_\ell(r,c)=0\). If \(c\ge r>0\) and \(p\) is an integer, then \(h_\ell(r,c)=0\). If \(p\) is not an integer and \(c\ge r>0\), then

\[
|h_\ell(r,c)|\le C_{p,d}c^p\ell^{-(2p+d-1)}. \tag{26}
\]

Consequently, for all sufficiently large \(\ell\), all \(r>0\), and all real \(c\),

\[
\ell^\Lambda|h_\ell(r,c)|\le C_{p,d}(r^p+|c|^p), \tag{27}
\]

where powers of order zero in this bound are read as one. Moreover, for fixed \(r\) with \(r\lt |c|\), the left side tends to zero as \(\ell\to\infty\).

**Proof.** The first two claims follow respectively from zero activation and polynomial orthogonality. For the remaining case put \(v=r/c\in(0,1]\) and

\[
Q_\ell(v)=\int_{-1}^1(1+vt)^pZ_\ell(t)\rho_d(t)dt.
\]

Rodrigues' formula and \(\ell\) integrations by parts, initially for \(v\lt 1\), show that \(Q_\ell(v)\) is a nonzero constant of fixed sign, depending only on \(p,d,\ell\), times

\[
\int_{-1}^1 v^\ell(1+vt)^{p-\ell}(1-t^2)^{\ell+\beta}dt. \tag{28}
\]

The constant contains the falling factorial \(p(p-1)\cdots(p-\ell+1)\); it is nonzero for noninteger \(p\). All boundary terms vanish because the endpoint orders before the last integration exceed zero. The logarithmic derivative in \(v\) of the positive factor in (28) has numerator \(\ell+pvt>0\), since \(\ell>p\). Thus \(|Q_\ell(v)|\) is nondecreasing in \(v\), and continuity by dominated convergence in the original integral extends this comparison to \(v=1\).

For \(b_0=(d-1)/2\), beta integration of the Jacobi polynomial gives the exact endpoint value

\[
Q_\ell(1)=\frac{2^pB(p+b_0,b_0)}{B(b_0,b_0)}
\frac{(-1)^\ell(-p)_\ell}{(p+d-1)_\ell}. \tag{29}
\]

For a direct verification, normalized Rodrigues gives the coefficient of the derivative of \((1-t^2)^{\ell+\beta}\) as \((-1)^\ell\Gamma(d/2)/(2^\ell\sqrt\pi\Gamma(\ell+\beta+1))\). After \(\ell\) integrations by parts at \(v=1\), the integral remaining is the beta integral of \((1-t)^{\ell+\beta}(1+t)^{p+\beta}\), multiplied by the falling factorial of \(p\). These integrations may be performed on truncated intervals and then continued to the endpoints: every boundary product at \(-1\) has order \(p+\beta+1>0\), and the final integrand has order \(p+\beta>-1\). The beta evaluation and \(p(p-1)\cdots(p-\ell+1)=(-1)^\ell(-p)_\ell\) give (29). The gamma ratio in (29) is \(O(\ell^{-(2p+d-1)})\) for noninteger \(p\). Since \(h_\ell(r,c)=c^pQ_\ell(v)\), (26) follows.

In the interior region (27) follows from (20) and (23), because \(\ell+\alpha\ge\ell\). In the exterior region, \(2p+d-1-\Lambda=p+d/2-1>0\) for noninteger \(p\ge0,d\ge2\). For integer \(p\) the coefficient there is exactly zero. This proves both (27) and the exterior pointwise limit, including the corner \(p=0,d=2\). At a boundary point \(|c|=r>0\), the same noninteger estimate is strictly smaller than \(\ell^{-\Lambda}\), while the integer coefficient is eventually zero. This observation also covers radial laws with endpoint atoms in Section 8. \(\square\)

### Lemma 9. Interior ridge asymptotic

For \(r>|c|\), define

\[
\theta_c(r)=\arccos(-c/r),\qquad
U_c(r)=\left(1-c^2/r^2\right)^{q/2},\qquad
\gamma=(q+1)\pi/2.
\]

Then

\[
\ell^\Lambda h_\ell(r,c)
=Hr^pU_c(r)\cos((\ell+\alpha)\theta_c(r)-\gamma)+o(1). \tag{30}
\]

For fixed \(r>0\), this is uniform whenever \(\theta_c(r)\) ranges over a compact subinterval of \((0,\pi)\). If \(c\) ranges over a fixed bounded set, the squares of the expressions in (27) have a common integrable bound under \(f_d(r)dr\).

**Proof.** Substitute (24) into (20) and use \(\ell/(\ell+\alpha)\to1\). For the last assertion, (27) is bounded by a constant times \(r^p+C^p\), and its square is integrable under (19). This explicitly controls the small-radius, threshold-endpoint, and large-radius regions in all subsequent limit interchanges. \(\square\)

## 5. Fixed thresholds: oscillatory averaging and cancellation

### Lemma 10. The Gaussian bilinear limit

For any two fixed real thresholds \(c,b\), along \((-1)^\ell=\varepsilon\),

\[
\ell^s\mathbb E[h_\ell(R_d,c)h_\ell(R_d,b)]
\longrightarrow
A e^{-c^2/2}\left[\mathbf1_{\{c=b\}}-
\varepsilon\cos(\pi p)\mathbf1_{\{c=-b\}}\right]. \tag{31}
\]

When \(c=b=0\), both indicators on the right are present.

**Proof.** The exterior pointwise limit in Lemma 8 and the common square-integrable majorant in Lemma 9 reduce the integral to \(r>\max(|c|,|b|)\). The error in replacing the two coefficients by (30) tends to zero in the radial integral: use boundedness and dominated convergence for the product error. Expanding the two cosines gives a difference phase and a sum phase, each with factor \(H^2/2\).

The difference phase is \((\ell+\alpha)(\theta_c-\theta_b)\). It is identically zero exactly when \(c=b\). The sum phase is \((\ell+\alpha)(\theta_c+\theta_b)-2\gamma\). If \(c=-b\), then \(\theta_c+\theta_b=\pi\), and its cosine is
\(\cos((\ell-p-1)\pi)=-\varepsilon\cos(\pi p)\).

There are no other nonoscillating contributions. Indeed,

\[
\theta_c'(r)=-\frac{c}{r\sqrt{r^2-c^2}}. \tag{32}
\]

For fixed admissible \(r\), the function \(c\mapsto c/\sqrt{r^2-c^2}\) is strictly increasing and odd. Hence the derivative of the difference phase divided by \(\ell+\alpha\) is nowhere zero if \(c\ne b\), and that of the sum phase is nowhere zero if \(c\ne-b\). On every compact radial interval strictly inside the integration region, change variables to the relevant phase; its continuous nonzero derivative gives an integrable transformed amplitude. The Riemann–Lebesgue lemma makes that integral vanish. The omitted radial endpoints and tail can be made arbitrarily small uniformly in \(\ell\), by (27), so the same holds on the full region.

For either resonant pair, the radial amplitude is

\[
\begin{split}
\mathbb E[R_d^{2p}U_c(R_d)^2\mathbf1_{\{R_d>|c|\}}]
&=\frac1{2^{d/2-1}\Gamma(d/2)}
\int_{|c|}^\infty r(r^2-c^2)^q e^{-r^2/2}dr\\
&=m e^{-c^2/2}. 
\end{split} \tag{33}
\]

The substitution \(u=(r^2-c^2)/2\) proves the last equality. Multiplication by \(H^2/2\) yields (31). \(\square\)

**Proof of Theorem 1.** Expand the square in (17) and apply (31) to its finitely many terms. This gives (3)–(5). To analyze zeros, use the weighted Euclidean inner product on the finite reflection-completed threshold set, with weights \(e^{-c^2/2}\). Reflection is an isometric involution, so \(|T|\le S\); equality \(T=\eta S\), \(\eta\in\{+1,-1\}\), holds precisely when the coefficient vector satisfies \(a_{-c}=\eta a_c\). For instance, the squared norm of \(a-\eta\mathcal Ra\) is \(2(S-\eta T)\), proving the equality condition directly.

If \(S>0\) and \(p\notin\mathbb Z\), \(|\cos\pi p|\lt 1\), so both constants are positive. If \(p\) is an integer, (4) vanishes exactly under (6). Let \(\psi=\sum_c a_c(t+c)_+^p\). The elementary identity

\[
u_+^p+(-1)^p(-u)_+^p=u^p \tag{34}
\]

holds everywhere for positive integer \(p\), and almost everywhere for \(p=0\). Under (6), reindexing the reflected sum gives

\[
\psi(t)+\varepsilon\psi(-t)=\sum_c a_c(t+c)^p. \tag{35}
\]

Thus the parity-\(\varepsilon\) part of \(\phi\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(\max(p,\deg P)\). Since \(Z_\ell(-t)=(-1)^\ell Z_\ell(t)\), its coefficient (16) vanishes above this degree on the stated parity, for every \(r>0\). Equation (17) then gives the exact zero branch. The other constant is \(2AS\). Finally, \(S=0\) forces every aggregate coefficient to be zero. These arguments prove exhaustiveness. \(\square\)

## 6. Spectral counting and the single-threshold limits

### Lemma 11. Counting the two harmonic parities

For either \(\varepsilon\in\{+1,-1\}\),

\[
\sum_{\substack{0\le\ell\le L\\(-1)^\ell=\varepsilon}}M_{\ell,d}
\sim\frac{L^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}. \tag{36}
\]

If a nonnegative sequence \(v_\ell\) satisfies \(\ell^sv_\ell\to b>0\) along one parity, then the corresponding weighted count of \(v_\ell>t\) is
\(b^\kappa t^{-\kappa}/(d-1)!\,(1+o(1))\).

**Proof.** For \(d\ge3\), (18) gives
\(M_{\ell,d}=2\ell^{d-2}/(d-2)!+O(\ell^{d-3})\), interpreted directly for \(d=3\). Summation on a progression of step two yields (36); for \(d=2\), count the integers of each parity and multiply by two. The possible degree-zero term has no effect. For the second assertion, fix \(0\lt \eta\lt b\). Outside finitely many degrees,
\((b-\eta)\ell^{-s}\le v_\ell\le(b+\eta)\ell^{-s}\).
Therefore the relevant count lies between the two counts with cutoffs \(((b-\eta)/t)^{1/s}\) and \(((b+\eta)/t)^{1/s}\), up to a finite error and endpoint choices. Apply (36), then let \(\eta\downarrow0\). This argument does not assume that \(v_\ell\) itself is monotone. \(\square\)

**Proof of Theorem 2.** Theorem 1 gives at least one positive branch. Each zero-constant branch is eventually exactly zero, so it contributes only a finite count. Apply Lemma 11 to the positive branches and add the two contributions. This proves the first statement in (8). To invert it, sandwich \(N(t)\) between \((D-\eta)t^{-\kappa}\) and \((D+\eta)t^{-\kappa}\) for small \(t\). Testing thresholds just below and above \(((D\pm2\eta)/n)^{1/\kappa}\) bounds the \(n\)-th ordered positive eigenvalue. Letting \(\eta\downarrow0\) gives the second statement, including tied eigenvalues. Finite exceptional multiplicities contribute only \(O(1)\) to the count. \(\square\)

**Proof of Corollary 4, fixed thresholds and iterated limits.** A single nonzero threshold has \(S=e^{-c^2/2}\), \(T=0\); a single zero threshold has \(S=T=1\). Theorems 1–2 give (12)–(13), and the integer cancellation follows from (34).

To justify the inner \(c\to0\) limit in (14), use the feature operators from Lemma 5. Isotropy gives

\[
\|F_c-F_0\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
=\mathbb E_{Z\sim N(0,1)}| (Z+c)_+^p-Z_+^p|^2\longrightarrow0. \tag{37}
\]

For \(p>0\) this is dominated convergence using a polynomial Gaussian envelope for bounded \(c\). For \(p=0\) the indicators converge away from \(Z=0\), a probability-zero event, and are bounded. The inequality
\(\|F_c^*F_c-F_0^*F_0\|_1\le(\|F_c\|_{\mathrm{HS}}+\|F_0\|_{\mathrm{HS}})\|F_c-F_0\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\)
implies convergence in operator norm. The variational characterization of compact positive-operator eigenvalues gives convergence of each ordered eigenvalue. All single-threshold kernels have infinite rank, so these are precisely the positive eigenvalue lists used above.

Finally, strict concavity of \(x^\kappa\), \(0\lt \kappa\lt 1\), gives
\((1-z)^\kappa+(1+z)^\kappa\lt 2\) for \(z\in[-1,1]\setminus\{0\}\), with equality at zero. Put \(z=\cos\pi p\) in (13). This proves (14) and its half-integer exception. At integer \(p\), the numerator at \(c=0\) is \(2^\kappa\), giving the stated ratio. \(\square\)

## 7. Moving thresholds and the transition matrix

**Proof of Theorem 3.** For a compact set of \(\tau\), choose \(B_0\lt \infty\) bounding every \(|\tau_j|\). Fix any \(r>0\). For sufficiently large \(\ell\), all thresholds \(\tau_j/\ell\) lie strictly inside \((-r,r)\). Uniformly for \(|\tau_j|\le B_0\),

\[
\theta_{\tau_j/\ell}(r)
=\frac\pi2+\frac{\tau_j}{\ell r}+O_{r,B_0}(\ell^{-3}),\qquad
U_{\tau_j/\ell}(r)=1+O_{r,B_0}(\ell^{-2}). \tag{38}
\]

Lemma 9, on a fixed compact interval about \(\pi/2\), therefore yields

\[
\ell^\Lambda h_\ell(r,\tau_j/\ell)
=Hr^p\cos\left(\frac{(\ell-p-1)\pi}{2}+\frac{\tau_j}{r}\right)+o(1), \tag{39}
\]

uniformly in the bounded threshold vector for this fixed \(r\). If \(\ell=2m\), the cosine is \((-1)^m\cos(\tau_j/r-\gamma_+)\). If \(\ell=2m+1\), it is \((-1)^m\cos(\tau_j/r-\gamma_-)\). The common sign disappears on squaring the sum.

The passage through the radial integral is uniform, not merely pointwise in \(\tau\). By (27), all scaled coefficients are bounded by \(C(r^p+B_0^p)\), for every sufficiently large \(\ell\), including radii below any moving threshold. The same bound, with a changed constant, dominates both the square of their finite weighted sum and the square of the limiting cosine sum. It is integrable under (19). For each fixed \(r>0\), the supremum over the compact parameter set of the difference of these squares tends to zero by (39). This supremum is measurable, since each coefficient is continuous in its threshold (including the step case by dominated convergence in (16)); it can equivalently be taken over a countable dense subset. Dominated convergence applied to this supremum proves the uniform convergence of the integrals.

Consequently their limit is

\[
H^2\mathbb E\left[R_d^{2p}
\left(\sum_j a_j\cos(\tau_j/R_d-\gamma_\varepsilon)\right)^2\right]. \tag{40}
\]

The tilted radial density satisfies

\[
\frac{r^{2p}f_d(r)}m=f_s(r),\quad s=d+2p, \tag{41}
\]

with \(f_s\) the chi density of real positive parameter \(s\). Since \(H^2m=2A\), expanding (40) gives (11) and (10). The product-to-sum identity, together with
\(2\gamma_+=(p+1)\pi\) and \(2\gamma_-=p\pi\), gives exactly (9), including its sign. Representation (11) proves positive semidefiniteness, and bounded dominated convergence proves continuity in the parameter vector. The polynomial term is absent from degree \(\ell\) once \(\ell>\deg P\). \(\square\)

**Completion of Corollary 4.** Take \(J=1\) and \(a_1=1\) in (9) to obtain (15). At \(\tau=0\), bounded convergence yields the factor \(\cos\pi p\). For large \(|\tau|\), the random variable \(U=1/R_s\) has the integrable density
\(f_s(1/u)/u^2\) on \(u>0\). The Riemann–Lebesgue lemma makes its characteristic function at \(2\tau\) tend to zero, proving the other transition endpoint. For integer \(p\), the branch that vanished at \(\tau=0\) becomes strictly positive for each \(\tau\ne0\): in (11) its scalar cosine or sine is not identically zero on the positive half-line, and \(U\) has strictly positive density there. This is a statement about the limiting branch at fixed nonzero \(\tau\), not a finite-sample detection theorem. \(\square\)

## 8. Arbitrary radial laws: continuous mass, atoms, and ranked spectra

The Gaussian law is useful for an explicit envelope, but it is not the boundary of the spectral method. In this section let \(w=Ru\), where \(u\sim\sigma\) is independent of a Borel probability law \(\nu\) for \(R\) on \([0,\infty)\). Take \(P=0\), and suppose only that

\[
m_\nu=\int_{(0,\infty)}r^{2p}\nu(dr)\lt\infty. \tag{42}
\]

For \(p=0\) this integral is the mass of the positive half-line. A mass at zero contributes only a constant kernel and is omitted from all positive-degree formulas. Polynomial perturbations remain harmless if their squared radial growth is also integrable. Write

\[
\nu|_{(0,\infty)}=\nu_{\mathrm c}+\sum_{j\in I}v_j\delta_{r_j}, \tag{43}
\]

where \(\nu_{\mathrm c}\) has no atoms, \(I\) is finite or countable, and \(v_j>0\). The continuous part may be singular with respect to Lebesgue measure. For a finite positive radial measure \(\xi\), define the even envelope

\[
B_\xi(c)=\frac{H^2}{2}\int_{r>|c|}r^{2p}\left(1-\frac{c^2}{r^2}\right)^q\xi(dr),\qquad
b_\varepsilon^{\mathrm c}=\sum_c\{a_c^2-\varepsilon\cos(\pi p)a_ca_{-c}\}B_{\nu_{\mathrm c}}(c). \tag{44}
\]

Both \(b_\varepsilon^{\mathrm c}\) are nonnegative by the same weighted reflection inequality used in Theorem 1.

For every pair \((j,c)\) with \(|c|\lt r_j\), set
\(\theta_{jc}=\arccos(-c/r_j)\), \(U_{jc}=(1-c^2/r_j^2)^{q/2}\), and \(\delta_{jc}=\alpha\theta_{jc}-\Lambda\pi/2\). Let

\[
g=\bigl(-1,(e^{i\theta_{jc}})_{j,c}\bigr),\qquad
G=\overline{\{g^n:n\in\mathbb Z\}}
\subset\{+1,-1\}\times\prod_{j,c}\mathbb S^1, \tag{45}
\]

and let \(m_G\) be Haar probability measure on this compact group. Empty coordinate products are allowed. For \(z=(\varepsilon,(z_{jc}))\in G\), put

\[
F(z)=b_\varepsilon^{\mathrm c}
+H^2\sum_{j\in I}v_jr_j^{2p}
\left[\sum_{|c|\lt r_j}a_cU_{jc}\operatorname{Re}(e^{i\delta_{jc}}z_{jc})\right]^2. \tag{46}
\]

This series converges uniformly by (42), so \(F\) is continuous, bounded, and nonnegative. The group in (45) retains all rational relations between the phases; replacing its coordinates by independent uniform angles would generally change the answer.

### Theorem 12. Radial decomposition and the exact ranked Weyl law

Under (42), the kernel is positive and trace class. Its harmonic eigenvalues satisfy

\[
\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac1L\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor L\rfloor}
\left|\ell^s\mu_\ell-F(g^\ell)\right|^2=0. \tag{47}
\]

Its positive spectral counting function has the exact limit

\[
\lim_{t\downarrow0}t^\kappa N(t)=D_\nu,\qquad
D_\nu=\frac{2}{(d-1)!}\int_G F(z)^\kappa\,m_G(dz). \tag{48}
\]

If \(D_\nu>0\), then \(\lambda_n\sim D_\nu^{1/\kappa}n^{-1/\kappa}\). If \(D_\nu=0\), the conclusion is only \(N(t)=o(t^{-\kappa})\), with no assertion of finite rank. In particular, if the positive radial law has no atoms, then

\[
D_\nu=\frac{(b_+^{\mathrm c})^\kappa+(b_-^{\mathrm c})^\kappa}{(d-1)!}. \tag{49}
\]

Formula (49) holds for singular continuous laws too, even when the scaled harmonic sequence does not converge on either parity. If \(\nu|_{(0,\infty)}\) has a Lebesgue density, (47) strengthens to the ordinary parity limits \(\ell^s\mu_\ell\to b_\varepsilon^{\mathrm c}\).

**Proof of (47).** The feature argument of Lemma 5 applies under (42), since a finite sum of shifted powers has squared growth bounded by \(C(1+r^{2p})\). For each fixed threshold, extend the leading expression in (30) by zero on \(r\le|c|\). Its difference from \(\ell^\Lambda h_\ell(r,c)\) tends to zero for every \(r\ge0\). At \(r=|c|>0\), this follows from (26): for noninteger \(p\), \(2p+d-1>\Lambda\); for integer \(p\), the coefficient is eventually exactly zero. At \(r=0\) every positive-degree coefficient is zero. Thus no endpoint atom is discarded. Bound (27) gives a common square-integrable majorant under \(\nu\). Replacing the coefficients in their integrated finite square therefore incurs an ordinary \(o(1)\) error. The atomic part of that square is exactly the series in (46) evaluated at \(g^\ell\).

For the nonatomic part, expand the products of cosines as in Lemma 10. The resonant difference and sum phases give (44). Every other phase \(\psi(r)=\theta_c(r)\pm\theta_b(r)\) is strictly monotone on \(r>\max(|c|,|b|)\), by (32). Its weighted pushforward modulo \(2\pi\) is a finite nonatomic complex measure: the phase has bounded range and each residue has only finitely many preimages. The fixed phase factors \(e^{i\alpha\psi}\) and constant offsets can be included in this measure.

Here is the finite-measure Fourier averaging fact being used. If \(\eta\) is a finite nonatomic complex measure on the circle and \(\widehat\eta(n)=\int e^{inx}\eta(dx)\), then

\[
\frac1L\sum_{n=1}^{L}|\widehat\eta(n)|^2
=\iint\left(\frac1L\sum_{n=1}^{L}e^{in(x-y)}\right)\eta(dx)\overline{\eta}(dy)
\longrightarrow0. \tag{50}
\]

The geometric average is bounded by one and tends to zero off the diagonal modulo \(2\pi\). That diagonal has zero \(|\eta|\times|\eta|\) measure because \(\eta\) has no atoms. Dominated convergence proves (50). This is the classical Wiener averaging mechanism [11], not a new Fourier theorem. Finite sums of these terms satisfy the same mean-square conclusion. Adding the ordinary \(o(1)\) ridge error proves (47). If a radial density exists, the change of variables and Riemann–Lebesgue argument in Lemma 10 instead makes each nonresonant term tend to zero ordinarily. No smoothness of that density is required. \(\square\)

### Lemma 13. Counting a continuous compact-group amplitude

Let \(G\) be a compact subgroup of a countable product of circles and a finite group, generated densely by \(g\). Let \(F:G\to[0,\infty)\) be continuous. If \(a_\ell\ge0\) is bounded and
\(L^{-1}\sum_{\ell\le L}|a_\ell-F(g^\ell)|^2\to0\), then the sequence \(v_\ell=\ell^{-s}a_\ell\), with harmonic multiplicities \(M_{\ell,d}\), satisfies (48).

**Proof.** We use the classical character proof of equidistribution [12]. A nontrivial finite-coordinate character on \(G\) has \(\chi(g)\ne1\); otherwise it is one on a dense set and hence on all of \(G\). Its average along \(g^\ell\) tends to zero by a geometric sum. The linear span of restrictions of finite-coordinate character monomials is a self-adjoint algebra separating points, so the Stone–Weierstrass theorem extends this conclusion to every continuous function, with the limit equal to its Haar integral. This also shows that positive powers have the required equidistribution on \(G\).

For every fixed \(C>0\), the empirical measures
\(L^{-1}\sum_{1\le\ell\le CL}\delta_{(\ell/L,g^\ell)}\)
converge weakly to Lebesgue measure on \([0,C]\) times \(m_G\). To see this first for a product test function, approximate its first factor by step functions; averages over each macroscopic interval follow by subtracting two initial-segment averages. Continuous product functions are uniformly dense, proving the assertion.

Put \(L=t^{-1/s}\). Choose \(C\) larger than both \(\sup_\ell a_\ell^{1/s}\) and \(\sup_z F(z)^{1/s}\). Boundedness permits the fixed cutoff \(\ell\le CL\), apart from finitely many degrees. For \(d\ge3\), use \(M_{\ell,d}=2\ell^{d-2}/(d-2)!+O(\ell^{d-3})\); the summed error is \(o(L^{d-1})\). For \(d=2\) the leading expression is the exact multiplicity two. The product measure of the boundary of \(\{(x,z):F(z)>x^s\}\) is zero: for each \(z\), there is at most one boundary value of \(x\). Thus the count for \(F(g^\ell)\) converges, after division by \(L^{d-1}\), to

\[
\frac{2}{(d-2)!}\int_G\int_0^{F(z)^{1/s}}x^{d-2}\,dx\,m_G(dz)
=\frac{2}{(d-1)!}\int_G F(z)^\kappa\,m_G(dz). \tag{51}
\]

To replace \(F(g^\ell)\) by \(a_\ell\), fix \(\eta>0\). Differences in the two indicators occur only when \(|a_\ell-F(g^\ell)|>\eta\) or \(|F(g^\ell)-(\ell/L)^s|\le\eta\). The first set has density tending to zero by the mean-square hypothesis and Chebyshev's inequality; the multiplicity weight is bounded by \(O(L^{d-2})\) within the cutoff. The second set has limiting weighted measure tending to zero as \(\eta\downarrow0\), by the same boundary-zero argument and bounded convergence. This proves (51) for \(a_\ell\), including the case of a zero integral. \(\square\)

**Completion of Theorem 12.** Bound (27) makes \(\ell^s\mu_\ell\) bounded. Apply Lemma 13 to (47), and invert the positive counting asymptotic when \(D_\nu>0\) as in Theorem 2. Without positive atoms the amplitude is \(b_\varepsilon^{\mathrm c}\); the parity coordinate has equal Haar masses, yielding (49). A zero constant cannot be upgraded to exact cancellation here: for example, a noninteger power with a positive threshold outside a bounded radial support can have a smooth nonpolynomial feature and smaller spectral order. The stronger exact-cancellation conclusion of Theorem 1 uses the strictly positive Gaussian envelope. \(\square\)

### Corollary 14. A single radial shell has arithmetic Weyl constants

Let \(d=2,p=0,R\equiv1\), \(-1\lt c\lt1\), and \(\theta=\arccos(-c)\). Direct integration gives

\[
\mu_\ell=\frac{\sin^2(\ell\theta)}{\pi^2\ell^2}\quad(\ell\ge1),\qquad M_{\ell,2}=2. \tag{52}
\]

Writing \(\lambda_n\sim W(c)n^{-2}\) for the ordered positive spectrum, one has

\[
W(c)=
\begin{cases}
\left[\dfrac{2}{\pi b}\cot\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2b}\right)\right]^2,
&\theta/\pi=a/b\text{ in lowest terms},\\[5pt]
16/\pi^4,&\theta/\pi\notin\mathbb Q.
\end{cases} \tag{53}
\]

**Proof.** The normalized circle coefficient is \(\pi^{-1}\int_0^\theta\cos(\ell t)dt\), proving (52). Lemma 13 gives \(D=2\pi^{-1}\operatorname{mean}|\sin(\ell\theta)|\), and \(W=D^2\). For rational \(a/b\), multiplication by \(a\) permutes the residues modulo \(b\), so this mean is
\(b^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{b-1}\sin(j\pi/b)=b^{-1}\cot(\pi/(2b))\), the last identity following by summing a geometric progression. For irrational \(\theta/\pi\), the circle mean is \(2/\pi\). This proves (53).

At \(c=1/2\), \(\theta=2\pi/3\): the scaled harmonic eigenvalues take the values zero and \(3/(4\pi^2)\), and neither parity converges. Nevertheless \(W=4/(3\pi^2)\). Averaging the scaled harmonic eigenvalues first and then applying the exponent \(\kappa=1/2\) would incorrectly give \(2/\pi^2\). Finally, \(x\cot x\lt1\) for \(0\lt x\lt\pi/2\), so every rational angle in (53) is a discontinuity point of the ranked leading constant when approached through irrational angles. This arithmetic dependence concerns the infinite-rank leading constant, despite continuity of each fixed ordered eigenvalue under changes of \(c\). \(\square\)

## 9. Coalescing thresholds determine the radial law

### Theorem 15. Universal transition matrices and deterministic identification

Under (42), the compact-uniform conclusion (10) holds with its right-hand matrix quadratic form replaced by

\[
\sum_{i,j}a_ia_jQ_\varepsilon(\tau_i,\tau_j),\qquad
Q_\varepsilon(u,v)=H^2\int_{(0,\infty)}r^{2p}
\cos(u/r-\gamma_\varepsilon)\cos(v/r-\gamma_\varepsilon)\nu(dr). \tag{54}
\]

For fixed known \(d,p\), the two matrix-valued transition functions determine the entire probability law \(\nu\), including any mass at zero. In fact their cross entries at \((u,0)\) suffice. This is an exact deterministic identification statement for a continuum of limiting spectral measurements, with no finite-sample rate or stability assertion.

**Proof.** The proof of Theorem 3 uses the Gaussian density only for integrability and for the final tilt (41). Condition (42) supplies the same bound \(C(1+r^{2p})\), so the measurable-supremum dominated convergence argument proves (54) unchanged. At \(r=0\) the feature is spatially constant and its positive-degree coefficient is zero, even for the moving step threshold. The matrices in (54) are continuous positive semidefinite Gram matrices.

Let \(\eta\) be the pushforward of the finite measure \(r^{2p}\nu(dr)|_{(0,\infty)}\) under \(r\mapsto1/r\). Since \(\gamma_+-\gamma_-=\pi/2\),

\[
C(u):=\frac{Q_+(u,0)+Q_-(u,0)}{H^2}
=\int_{(0,\infty)}\cos(uz)\,\eta(dz). \tag{55}
\]

The cosine transform uniquely determines a finite measure on the positive half-line. For completeness, symmetrize it to a measure on \(\mathbb R\); (55) becomes its full Fourier transform. If two such transforms agree, convolving their difference with a Gaussian gives zero by Fourier inversion, since the Gaussian multiplier is integrable and the transform is bounded. Letting the Gaussian variance decrease to zero shows equality against every continuous compactly supported test function, hence equality of the measures. Restricting to the positive half-line recovers \(\eta\).

The original law is then recovered by the exact inverse weighting

\[
\nu(E)=\int_{(0,\infty)}\mathbf1_{\{1/z\in E\}}z^{2p}\eta(dz)
\quad(E\subset(0,\infty)),\qquad
\nu\{0\}=1-\int z^{2p}\eta(dz). \tag{56}
\]

The last integral is finite because it equals the original positive radial mass. No negative radial moment is assumed. Cross entries in (54) can be recovered by polarization from the limits for coefficient vectors \((1,1),(1,0),(0,1)\), which are within the finite-threshold family. Thus (55) describes available limiting spectral data, not an additional unobserved operator. \(\square\)

For comparison, an unshifted homogeneous activation obeys \(K_\nu=m_\nu K_{R=1}\), so it records only one radial moment. The threshold transition family resolves this loss of radial information for every real \(p\ge0\). Fourier uniqueness, polarization, and inverse weighting are classical; the content here is their exact realization through the high-degree deterministic-threshold spectrum, including arbitrary atomic and singular continuous radial laws.


## 10. Checks, scope, and interpretation

Several elementary cases specify the normalization independently of an asymptotic fit. For \(d=2,p=0,c=0\), (16) is
\(h_\ell=\sin(\ell\pi/2)/(\pi\ell)\) for \(\ell\ge1\), so \(\mu_\ell=h_\ell^2\); this agrees with (12) and the circle multiplicity two. For \(d=3,p=1,c=0\), \(Z_2(t)=(3t^2-1)/2\) and \(h_2(r,0)=r/16\), hence \(\mu_2=3/256\). The latter is a low-degree check and is not predicted by replacing an asymptotic equivalence with a finite-degree equality.

For the symmetric ReLU pair \((t+b)_++(t-b)_+\), \(b>0\), the odd component is linear, so all odd degrees above one vanish exactly; (4) gives the even constant \(4Ae^{-b^2/2}\). Reversing the sign between the two terms makes the high even branch vanish. These examples show why opposite-threshold interference cannot be discarded. For Gaussian radii, at noninteger powers no nonzero fixed threshold combination in this common-order class has a vanishing leading parity constant.

The companion reproducibility material evaluates the defining ridge integrals and radial averages on a finite parameter grid, compares available exact cases, and compares increasing-degree values with (4) and (9). Additional exact shell and Gaussian-shell mixture checks test the arithmetic counting law (53) and the Haar amplitude in (48). Such numerical checks test signs and normalization; they do not prove the all-parameter bounds, infinite-degree limits, or compact uniformity. Those claims follow from the analytic arguments above.

The scale \(c=\tau/\ell\) is a spectral scale. Formula (10) does not by itself yield a statistical threshold for estimating bias from finitely many neurons or stimuli. Fixed-parameter ranked laws and the degree-dependent transition law also have different quantifiers. The half-integer exception in (14) concerns the ranked leading constants; it does not make the full matrix function (9) constant. Finally, the common real power and the finite threshold set are part of the theorem: different singularity orders, growing numbers of knots, and nonuniform sphere measures require separate analysis. General radial laws are covered by Sections 8–9 under (42); the zero-amplitude case in (48) deliberately leaves the finer spectral order open. Exact continuum identification in Theorem 15 does not imply robust recovery from a finite or noisy set of transition measurements.
