Fractional neural kernels: radial spectral laws, threshold interference, and inverse limits

Abstract

We study random-feature kernels on a probability sphere generated by finite sums of shifted positive-part powers. For Gaussian weights we determine exact harmonic and ranked spectral constants, classify polynomial parity cancellations, and resolve Conjecture 1 of Schmutz et al. (2025). We then prove a radial-distribution structure theorem under only the natural activation moment condition. The nonatomic radial part contributes deterministic parity amplitudes in mean square, even for singular continuous laws; each radial atom retains an oscillatory amplitude. The ranked Weyl constant is the Haar average of a fractional power of the resulting continuous function on the compact group generated by all atomic phases. This law remains valid when no harmonic-parity limit exists and yields explicit arithmetic discontinuities for a single radial shell. At inverse-degree threshold scaling, we prove compact-uniform Gram limits for arbitrary radial measures and show that their two parity families determine the radial law exactly. The proof combines classical fractional Jacobi estimates, Wiener averaging, and compact-group equidistribution. The new structure is the radial decomposition of threshold spectra and their inverse spectral realization, rather than new special-function or transform-inversion methods.

Schmutz et al. [1, equations (9)–(12), Conjecture 1] ask for a positive finite ranked spectral limit for the single term (t+c)+p(t+c)_+^p, under exactly the Gaussian-weight and probability-sphere conventions in (1). Their symbol for asymptotic comparison is explicitly defined as a ratio with a positive finite limit, a stronger assertion than two-sided order bounds. Their interpretation of the decay exponent and the resulting ambiguity between activation regularity and latent dimension are prior results and motivations, not new statistical identifiability claims here.

The harmonic method and the relation between singularities and spectra are established tools. Bach [2] treats homogeneous integer powers and their parity structure. Bietti and Bach [3, Theorem 7] transfer endpoint power singularities of fixed zonal kernels into harmonic asymptotics. Xie et al. [4, Appendix 3.3, equations A32 and A34] use Gaussian radial averaging and Rodrigues formulas for a deterministic-threshold ReLU model. Bordelon and Pehlevan [5, Appendix, “Nonlinear simple cells”] discuss the threshold-independent power of the spectral tail in a circular Gabor model. None of these ingredients is claimed as new.

Li et al. [6, Theorems 8 and 10] transfer known spherical eigenvalue orders to more general domains; those order results do not supply the moving-threshold limits considered here. Holzmüller and Schölpple [7, Assumption 4 and Remarks 8 and A.7] analyze activations with their only nonsmooth point at zero, and discuss extensions to other locations. Liu and Wang [8, equation (2.7), Theorems 2.1–2.2] already provide fractional Gegenbauer identities, oscillatory constants, and weighted endpoint estimates. We give a convenient self-contained estimate for the needed cap integral, while crediting that established special-function analysis. The exact Ferrers and Jacobi representations used below are classical [9].

Tsuchida, Roosta, and Gallagher [10, Proposition 1] already establish zero-bias ReLU kernel invariance for rotationally invariant weight densities with finite second moment. More generally, homogeneity makes the zero-bias radial dependence a single moment. Thus neither the use of non-Gaussian weights nor this homogeneous invariance is new. Wiener averaging and equidistribution are classical [11,12]; we give the exact elementary forms needed below. The fixed-bias envelope is also a Williamson-type integral transform: at positive integer orders its inversion belongs to established theory [13, Proposition 3.1], not to the novelty claimed here.

The Gaussian results provide a complete solution of the specified source conjecture and an exact finite-threshold interference law. The further contribution is a structure theorem for arbitrary radial measures, including singular continuous and countably atomic laws: it identifies which oscillations disappear in density, which survive as compact-group amplitudes, and how those amplitudes determine the ranked spectral constant. The inverse-degree transition family additionally identifies the radial measure that zero-bias homogeneous kernels cannot distinguish. These are infinite-width population-kernel results. They do not assert finite-width concentration, finite-sample identification rates or stability, nonuniform-input laws, or corresponding theorems for deep networks. The literature comparison is bounded by the identified sources and does not establish worldwide priority.

10. Checks, scope, and interpretation

Several elementary cases specify the normalization independently of an asymptotic fit. For d=2,p=0,c=0d=2,p=0,c=0, (16) is hℓ=sin(ℓπ/2)/(πℓ)h_\ell=\sin(\ell\pi/2)/(\pi\ell) for ℓ≥1\ell\ge1, so μℓ=hℓ2\mu_\ell=h_\ell^2; this agrees with (12) and the circle multiplicity two. For d=3,p=1,c=0d=3,p=1,c=0, Z2(t)=(3t2−1)/2Z_2(t)=(3t^2-1)/2 and h2(r,0)=r/16h_2(r,0)=r/16, hence μ2=3/256\mu_2=3/256. The latter is a low-degree check and is not predicted by replacing an asymptotic equivalence with a finite-degree equality.

For the symmetric ReLU pair (t+b)++(t−b)+(t+b)_++(t-b)_+, b>0b>0, the odd component is linear, so all odd degrees above one vanish exactly; (4) gives the even constant 4Ae−b2/24Ae^{-b^2/2}. Reversing the sign between the two terms makes the high even branch vanish. These examples show why opposite-threshold interference cannot be discarded. For Gaussian radii, at noninteger powers no nonzero fixed threshold combination in this common-order class has a vanishing leading parity constant.

The companion reproducibility material evaluates the defining ridge integrals and radial averages on a finite parameter grid, compares available exact cases, and compares increasing-degree values with (4) and (9). Additional exact shell and Gaussian-shell mixture checks test the arithmetic counting law (53) and the Haar amplitude in (48). Such numerical checks test signs and normalization; they do not prove the all-parameter bounds, infinite-degree limits, or compact uniformity. Those claims follow from the analytic arguments above.

The scale c=τ/ℓc=\tau/\ell is a spectral scale. Formula (10) does not by itself yield a statistical threshold for estimating bias from finitely many neurons or stimuli. Fixed-parameter ranked laws and the degree-dependent transition law also have different quantifiers. The half-integer exception in (14) concerns the ranked leading constants; it does not make the full matrix function (9) constant. Finally, the common real power and the finite threshold set are part of the theorem: different singularity orders, growing numbers of knots, and nonuniform sphere measures require separate analysis. General radial laws are covered by Sections 8–9 under (42); the zero-amplitude case in (48) deliberately leaves the finer spectral order open. Exact continuum identification in Theorem 15 does not imply robust recovery from a finite or noisy set of transition measurements.
