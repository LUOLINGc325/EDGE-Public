Sharp deficiency and the size of least-favourable restrictions for Poisson signals

Abstract

A Poisson count with a large known background can convey bounded information about a signal whose clean count becomes arbitrarily informative. We determine the first-order gap between the one-sided deficiency and one. Let nn be the largest clean intensity, AnA_n the source background mean, and BnB_n the source exposure multiplier. If An−1/2=o(logn/n)A_n^{-1/2}=o(\sqrt{\log n/n}) and Bnn/An→κ∈[0,∞)B_n n/\sqrt{A_n}\to\kappa\in[0,\infty), the gap is cκlogn/n(1+o(1))c_\kappa\sqrt{\log n/n}(1+o(1)). The latter ratio measures the source signal range in background standard deviations. A scalar boundary-value equation gives cκc_\kappa, and its solution specifies an asymptotically attaining simulator and a matching prior bound. More generally the source can follow a nondecreasing continuously differentiable path of exponential tilts of a positive continuous density on ℝ\mathbb R, with its moment-generating function finite on an open interval containing the tilt range. The worst deficiency on at most kk parameter states tends to 1−1/k1-1/k for each fixed kk. To recover the full deficiency within a prescribed multiple of its gap from one, the number of states must grow on the order of n/logn\sqrt{n/\log n}. A separate finite-intensity example has an exact two-state least-favourable restriction with one interior intensity.

Guide for readers

Imagine measuring a weak signal on top of a large, known background. A simulator sees one noisy count and tries to produce a count distributed as if it came from the clean signal. The deficiency is the smallest worst-case discrepancy that any simulator can achieve. A deficiency near one means that even the best simulator has very little uniform overlap with the clean count law.

The main theorem tells us how much overlap remains to first order as the largest clean intensity grows. Using the square root of intensity makes the natural comparison windows approximately equal in width. Section 2 constructs a simulator whose local output density is equal at every interior signal level and a prior that matches it in the limit. Sections 3 and 4 turn that calculation into matching bounds for Poisson counts, including signals close to zero. The finite-state results explain how densely the signal interval must be sampled to detect the same discrepancy.

A.6. Relation to binary Poisson comparison

Thinning plus independent Poisson superposition is a classical comparison for ordered signal and background parameters, as in Laourine and Wagner, Lemma 1. Binary testing characterizations of Poisson experiment comparison appear in Pandey, Maleki and Kulkarni, Sections 2.1 and 4, Lemma 21 and Theorem 13. Here the signal increase and background increase point in opposite directions, and the deficiency is positive. A new issue is to justify a single kernel across a continuum of intensity values. The finite positive-output distribution makes this possible, while the worst-state proof selects an interior intensity. In a first-moment fiber on [0,1][0,1], every uu occurs as the mean and the family can equivalently be indexed by the underlying mixing law. The pair here is the clean unit-exposure limit and the competing source with exposure multiplier two and total background mean two. The local persistence result does not classify all exposure and background regimes.
